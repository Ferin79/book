## The Author

**Ferin Patel** — son of Rakesh Patel, writing this book together with his sister's account and his own lived experience.

**Point of view**: First person. Ferin Patel is the narrator throughout. His sister Janvi Patel's account is incorporated as her experience within his narration (clearly attributed to her when it's her perspective specifically, not presented as his own memory).

## The Premise

This book is about **Rakesh Patel** — a man who, in the eyes of society, is the model of a modern, devout, caring Hindu family man. In the eyes of his own children, he failed at the most basic duty of a father: he consistently placed his personal pride and his standing in society above his wife and his children.

The book's central argument: **a person who performs virtue in public while causing real harm in private is more dangerous to society than someone who is openly flawed** — because the performance is what earns him trust, respect, and protection, while the harm goes unseen and unaddressed. This book exists to close that gap between the image Rakesh Patel has built and the reality his family lived.

This is not a book of abstract accusation. It is built from specific, concrete incidents — from his own childhood, through his youth, through his marriage, through raising his children — because specific truth is what breaks a false public image; general condemnation does not.

## The Wider Mission — Why This Is Bigger Than One Family

This book tells one family's story, but it is written to be **more than one family's story.** The author's explicit intent is for this book to function as a role-model / eye-opening work for the many households across India that live behind a similar authoritative, image-conscious figure — someone who performs modernity, faith, and care in public while a spouse or children absorb a very different reality at home.

**What this book aims to give readers who recognize themselves in it:**
- **Recognition** — the specific, named experience of naming what happens behind closed doors, so readers trapped in a similar dynamic see their own experience reflected and understand they are not alone or imagining it.
- **A vocabulary for what's actually happening** — using the same scripture their own household authority figure may invoke, but restored to its actual meaning, so readers can see clearly the difference between performed dharma and lived dharma.
- **Encouragement to raise their voice** — against unjust treatment inside a household that outwardly looks respectable, and often is protected by that respectability.
- **A reinforcement of the pillars this book stands for**: freedom (from a false, imposed image), righteousness (dharma as it's actually defined, not as it's performed), the health of the family unit, and the health of the society those families make up.

### Who This Book Is For
- **Primary**: Ferin Patel, Janvi Patel, and their own reckoning with their father — the book exists first for them.
- **Secondary**: Readers — especially in India — currently living under, or having grown up under, a household figure who performs virtue publicly while causing private harm; anyone who has struggled to make others believe a reality that contradicts a convincing public image.
- **Tertiary**: General readers of memoir and social commentary interested in the intersection of patriarchy, religious performance, and family harm in Indian society.

### Reconciling the Personal and the Universal (craft note)
The temptation with a book that wants to be a "role model for others" is to have the narrator step outside the story and lecture the reader directly, chapter after chapter. **Resist this inside the narrative chapters** — per the voice rules below, the specific incident, told honestly, is what readers recognize themselves in; a general statement about "many households in India" is not something a reader feels, it's something they're told, and it will read as diluting the personal account into a pamphlet.

The universal message should instead come through three channels that don't require breaking the memoir's voice:
1. **The specificity itself.** The more precisely Rakesh Patel's mask and its cost are documented, the more other readers will recognize the pattern in their own household — universality through precision, not through generalization.
2. **The scripture.** Because the verses used are widely known across Indian households, they give readers a shared, culturally resonant standard for recognizing when "dharma" is being invoked to justify harm rather than prevent it — this is the framework's real power beyond indicting one man.
3. **Explicit bookend material.** An **Author's Note** (front matter, before Part I) and/or an **Afterword** (back matter, after the final part) are the appropriate places for Ferin Patel to speak directly to readers about the book's wider purpose and offer solidarity/encouragement — direct address belongs at the edges of the book, not woven through every chapter.

## Author's Intent (in the author's own words, lightly organized)

- To show Rakesh Patel's true nature — not to invent a worse version of him, but to document the real one, incident by incident.
- To draw a clear, sustained contrast between his carefully maintained public image (modern, religious, caring) and his private conduct within the family.
- To make the case — through his own life and specific incidents — that this kind of person (performing dharma while violating it at home) causes more harm than someone who never claimed virtue at all.
- To use classical Hindu scripture — texts Rakesh Patel's own public persona is built on claiming to honor — as the moral and rhetorical measuring stick against which his private actions are held.

## A Note on Honesty and Craft

The strongest version of this book does not need the narrator to editorialize or call Rakesh Patel names. Per this project's voice rules (`references/author_rules.md`, `references/narrative_nonfiction_rules.md`):

- **Show the incident. Let it carry the weight.** The facts, told specifically and honestly, are the argument.
- **No hagiography, no character assassination.** Where something genuinely complicates the picture (a moment of real care, a redeeming context), it is not omitted for the sake of the thesis — the honest, complicated truth lands harder and is harder to dismiss than a one-sided portrait.
- **The scripture does the moral commentary the narrator doesn't have to.** This is precisely why the reference framework below matters so much: it lets a verse on true dharma sit next to a real incident and make the contradiction self-evident, without the author having to argue it.
- **Accuracy is the book's armor.** Rakesh Patel has already convinced people of his version. The only way to overturn that is if every fact, date, and quoted verse in this book is exactly right — a single inaccuracy gives his defenders a reason to dismiss the rest. Flag anything uncertain with `[NEED RESEARCH]` rather than guess.

## Proposed Book Structure — Organized by Relationship

Per the author's direction, the book is organized by relationship/theme rather than strict chronology, though each part moves through time internally. This is a proposed shape for discussion, not a locked outline — chapter-level outlining happens separately in `Outlines/`.

**Author's Note** *(front matter, working addition — confirm with author)*
A short direct address, before Part I, situating the book: this is one family's true story, and it is offered in solidarity with others living a similar reality. States the book's wider intent without diluting the memoir chapters that follow with repeated direct address.

**Part I — The Mask**
Rakesh Patel as society sees him: modern, devout, faithful to Hindu tradition, caring. Establishes the public image in full before it's dismantled — the reader needs to believe in the mask before watching it come apart.

**Part II — Where It Began**
His birth, his youth, and his relationship with his own father, Sukhdevbhai Patel (and mother, Nandanben Patel). Not an excuse for what came later, but the honest context of how he was shaped.

**Part III — The Husband**
His relationship with Heena Patel — the marriage as it was actually lived, behind the version presented to relatives and neighbors.

**Part IV — The Father**
His relationship with his children — Ferin and Janvi. The incidents at the center of the book's argument.

**Part V — The Reckoning** *(working placeholder — confirm with author)*
Where the book lands: the cost of the mask, and the argument that this kind of hidden harm is more damaging than open failure.

**Afterword — To Anyone Still Inside It** *(back matter, working addition — confirm with author)*
Direct address to readers who recognize their own household in this book: naming the pattern, encouragement to raise their voice, and the book's closing case for freedom, real (not performed) righteousness, and the health of the family and society built on them.

## The Scripture & Reference Framework

> **This is a load-bearing part of this book. Use these references as much as the text can bear.** They are not decoration — they are the moral vocabulary the book uses instead of the narrator's own judgment. Every major incident should be considered for a scriptural counterpoint.

### Approved Sources
- **Shreemad Bhagavad Gita**
- **Mahabharata** (the epic itself, beyond just the Gita)
- **Valmiki Ramayana**
- **Chanakya Niti / Arthashastra**
- **The Vedas** (Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, Atharvaveda — cite the specific one)

The logic: Rakesh Patel's public identity is built on being "truly faithful to Hindu religion." These are the texts that religion is actually built on. Holding his private conduct up against their own definitions of dharma, a father's duty, honesty, and right action is the most direct way to show the gap between his claim and his conduct — using his own claimed standard against him, not an external or invented one.

### Reference Format (confirmed)

Every reference follows this exact structure — Sanskrit verse, source citation, translation, then the author's own analysis connecting it to the specific incident. Use a Markdown blockquote (indented) for the verse itself so it visually stands apart from the narrative prose.

**Template:**

> देवनागरी में मूल श्लोक यहाँ ।
> दूसरी पंक्ति यहाँ ॥
> — *Source Title, Chapter/Book X, Verse Y*
>
> **Translation:** "Plain-English translation of the verse."

Followed immediately (outside the blockquote) by a short paragraph of the author's own analysis connecting the verse to the incident just described or about to be described.

**Worked example (the author's own):**

> यदा यदा हि धर्मस्य ग्लानिर्भवति भारत ।
> अभ्युत्थानमधर्मस्य तदात्मानं सृजाम्यहम् ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 4, Verse 7*
>
> **Translation:** "Whenever there is a decline in righteousness and an increase in unrighteousness, O Bharata, at that time I manifest Myself on earth."

*(Analysis would follow here in the actual chapter, tying the verse to the specific incident it's paired with.)*

### Format Rules
- **Devanagari + translation + analysis only.** No transliteration line, no traditional commentator excerpt — keep it to the verse, its meaning, and the author's own point (per the author's explicit preference).
- **Always cite precisely**: source text, chapter/book, and verse number (e.g., *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 4, Verse 7*; *Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda, Sarga 21*; *Chanakya Niti, Chapter 3, Verse 2*).
- **Verify before finalizing.** Sanskrit is unforgiving of small errors, and a wrong verse or citation undermines the book's credibility exactly where it needs to be strongest. Any verse not yet checked against a reliable edition should be marked `[VERIFY VERSE]` in the draft, the same way uncertain facts are marked `[NEED RESEARCH]`.
- **No fabricated or paraphrased-as-if-quoted verses.** If the precise verse can't be confirmed, use `[VERIFY VERSE: description of what's needed]` rather than presenting an approximate or invented line as scripture.

### Placement (decided per chapter)
- **As a chapter epigraph** — sets the moral frame before the reader sees the incident.
- **Inline at the moment of the incident** — placed right after (or interleaved with) the scene it comments on, for direct, ironic contrast (e.g., right after describing him performing a puja or giving a public speech on values, followed by the verse on what that value actually requires).
- Decide per chapter based on which lands harder for that specific incident — there's no fixed rule; both are in active use across the book.

## Sensitivity Note

This book makes serious, specific claims about a real, named, living person. That's the entire point of the book — but it also means:
- Keep whatever factual backup exists for each incident (dates, your sister's corroboration, any documentation) tracked alongside the incident, especially given the author's stated intent for this book to eventually reach readers beyond the family, not stay purely private.
- The private-repository setting means real names are fine to use throughout the writing process. If and when publication is pursued, revisit names, identifying details, and legal review with the author at that point — that's a decision for that stage, not now.
- Because the goal is to be believed by strangers who don't already know this family, the same accuracy discipline that protects the book from Rakesh Patel's defenders (see "A Note on Honesty and Craft" above) is what will make it land as truth for readers who see their own household in it, rather than as an aggrieved child's complaint.
