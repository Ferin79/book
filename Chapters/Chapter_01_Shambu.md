# Part I — The Mask

## Chapter One: Shambu

Every story I can tell about my father starts the same way other people's memories of him do, and not mine: with a name that wasn't the one his parents gave him.

On Moti Fadiyu, the street where he grew up, in the village of Pal Gam, nobody used his real name.

"Shambu, kem che?" a neighbor would call from a doorway, and he'd lift a hand without slowing down, already halfway to wherever he was going. Uncles used it. Aunties who had known him since before he could walk used it. The boys he'd grown up with still used it, years later, without a second thought. His name was Rakesh Patel. The street had given him another one, and it had nothing to do with what his parents had chosen and everything to do with a single fact: he was born on a Monday, the second of August, 1976, and it was the first Monday of Shravan.

To anyone raised the way he was, that sentence needs no footnote. Every Monday in Shravan month belongs to Shiva. Households fast for it. People walk to the temple for it. They wait all year for the first one to come around so the observance can begin again. A boy born on that particular Monday doesn't get named so much as claimed. Shambhu is one of Shiva's own names, and the street picked it up the way a nickname settles on a child before anyone has decided whether he'll grow into it.

He grew into it.

---

Surat runs on diamonds and cloth, on people who came from somewhere smaller and worked their way toward something bigger. Pal Gam, the part of it my father was born and raised in, was smaller still: narrow streets, close houses, everyone's business folded into everyone else's. He was the son of a farmer and a housewife, neither of whom had ever had the chance at an education themselves. His father, Sukhdevbhai Patel, worked land that never made anyone rich. His mother, Nandanben Patel, ran a household that had to be managed carefully, because there wasn't much room in it for anything else. My father's childhood, and then his teenage years, were spent inside that carefulness: poor first, and lower middle class only once the family had worked hard enough to earn the upgrade.

He rarely talked about those years in any detail, not even with us. What he did say, on the occasions he said anything at all, was how hard it had been to pay his own way through college. His parents couldn't help him with it, not because they didn't want to, but because neither of them had ever been taught how any of it worked, let alone had the money to carry him through it. Exactly what he went without, what corners he cut, whose help he leaned on and for how long — none of that ever made it into the stories he told his own children. What made it into the stories was simpler: it was hard, and he did it anyway.

By the time he was finished, he had two things almost nobody around him had. A law degree, and English. In Pal Gam, in those years, either one on its own would have counted as an achievement for a farmer's son. He had both.

---

On the tenth of December, 1998, he married my mother, Heena Patel. I was born the following year, on the ninth of December. My sister, Janvi, followed on the eleventh of November, 2002. By then he had settled into steady work in accounting, in the same city where he'd once been too poor to imagine having any work at all. Two children, one wife, and a job he could count on — the shape of a life he had built himself, more or less from nothing.

He made sure of one thing before almost anything else. Whatever his own schooling had cost him, in money he didn't have and years he could still describe better than he could explain, his own children were not going to pay that same price. My sister and I were admitted into English medium school, not the Gujarati medium school most families on that street still sent their children to. In Surat, in those years, an English medium seat wasn't only an admission. It was a small, deliberate announcement: this family had decided where its children were going, whatever it took to get them there. He never explained the decision out loud, not that either of us remembers him doing. He didn't need to. He was the boy who had taught himself English out of necessity, one exam at a time, while his own parents couldn't so much as check his homework. We were not going to start that far back. It was, in its way, the same instinct that had made him a good son to two people who'd had nothing to give him but their blessing: he never held the lack against them. He simply made sure it stopped with him.

The street's opinion of him never wavered. He was honest, people said, in a place where that word got handed out carefully and not to everyone. He worked hard. And every morning and every evening, without any fuss made about it, he prayed — the same devotion that had put "Shambu" in everyone's mouth before he was a day old, except by now it belonged to him. Not a name he'd been given, but a practice he'd chosen, twice a day, for years on end.

That devotion, and that reputation, were only two threads of him. There was a third: for as long as anyone could remember, some part of him had one eye on somewhere else.

He was drawn to foreign countries the way some people are drawn to a particular sound, without a clean explanation for it. What turned the daydream into a plan was a work trip: a chance to travel to Hong Kong, in China, for his job. He came back from that trip decided in a way he hadn't been before. He had seen, if only briefly, what a life outside Surat could look like, and it settled something in him that had been loose for years. He wanted to build a life for his family somewhere in North America.

By the time he was 30, he had sat the IELTS exam more than once and applied, through every legitimate channel he could find, for permanent residency in both the United States and Canada. It took years: paperwork, fees, and the particular kind of hope that has to be renewed every time it's disappointed. None of it worked out. The one opportunity that would have moved his family across an ocean never arrived.

So he stayed in Surat. He kept the job in accounting. He kept praying morning and evening to the god whose name the street had given him before he could talk. And by every account of the people who knew him at the time, he was happy, genuinely and without performance, with the life he had instead: a wife, two children, and a name the whole street still used without thinking twice.

---

I've heard the Hong Kong story more than once, and the IELTS story too, always folded together the same way: proof that he tried, and proof that trying wasn't always enough. There's never a complaint attached to either telling. Not one I've ever caught, anyway. That's the part I've never quite been able to explain — a man who wanted out that badly, who didn't get to leave, and who seemed, by every measure available to a child watching him, content anyway.

Say his name on Moti Fadiyu today and people still light up. *Shambu.* Not Rakesh. Never Rakesh. For the first thirty years of his life, on that street and nearly everywhere else, the name fit him exactly.

---

Moti Fadiyu was Kadva Patel *Samaj*, every house on it, and so was the rest of Pal Gam. But the Samaj didn't end at the village line. It was a caste network that had bound Patel families across Gujarat for generations, running everything from a neighborhood wedding to its own schools and hospitals, and in it, my father was known by far more people than just the ones who lived near him, and thought of no differently.

Weddings needed someone to handle the arrangements before the guests arrived, and he showed up. Funerals needed someone steady enough to stand at the front and manage what nobody else wanted to think about, and he was there. Every festival that came through the street, and in that neighborhood they came through often, found him already involved rather than watching from the side. When the *Samaj* needed something organized, someone consulted, or a disagreement quietly settled before it turned into something worse, his name came up before most others did.

The picture people built of him out of all that held up under any kind of scrutiny, too. He didn't drink. He didn't smoke. He was fully vegetarian, the kind of detail that, in that community, said as much about discipline as it did about diet. He was trustworthy, with money, with responsibility, with another family's daughter at her own wedding. Somewhere across all those weddings and funerals and festivals and quiet favors, he had earned an actual place in that *Samaj*: the kind most men spend a lifetime chasing and never quite get.

That pull didn't run only one direction, either. He was just as often the one looking for the next function to help with, the next committee that could use a name, the next festival that could use one more person standing at the front instead of watching from the back. Whether a particular way of doing something in the *Samaj* was good for a family or not was never really a question he seemed to ask, out loud or otherwise. If everyone around him believed something was simply how things were done, that was reason enough for it to be right for his own house too. He trusted it completely, the same unquestioning way he trusted the calendar that had named him.

None of that held itself up for free.

A place like that, a name said with that much warmth, isn't handed to a man for showing up a few times. Someone pays for it, continuously, in a currency the street never sees change hands. I didn't understand that as a child, watching him get called away, again, to arrange someone else's wedding or lead someone else's *puja*. I understood it later, and even then only partly: that the man the whole *Samaj* trusted without question had been paying for that trust the entire time, in ways none of us saw and he never once mentioned.

I still don't know everything that price included. I know what was riding on it, because I watched it up close from the time I was old enough to notice: his pride. His place in that *Samaj*. Every ounce of a respect it had taken a poor farmer's son a lifetime to build. All of it stood on something he could not afford to stop paying, and for a long time, he didn't.
