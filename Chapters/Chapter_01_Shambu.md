# Chapter One: Shambu

Every story about my father starts the same way. Not with his real name, but with a different one.

On Moti Fali, the street where he grew up, in the village of Pal Gam, nobody called him by his real name.

"Shambu, kem che?" a neighbor would call from a doorway. He'd wave back without slowing down, already halfway to somewhere else. Uncles called him Shambu. Aunties who'd known him since he was a baby called him Shambu. Even the boys he grew up with still called him that, years later, without a second thought. His real name was Rakesh Patel. The street gave him a different one, and it wasn't random. He was born on a Monday, August 2, 1976. That day was the first Monday of Shravan.

Anyone raised the way he was already understands why that matters. Every Monday in Shravan month belongs to Shiva. Families fast for it. People walk to the temple for it. They wait all year for the first one to come back around. A boy born on that day doesn't just get named. He gets claimed. Shambhu is one of Shiva's own names. The street picked it up the same way a nickname sticks to a child, before anyone knows if it will fit him.

It fit him.

---

Surat is a city built on diamonds and cloth. It's full of people who came from somewhere small and worked their way toward something bigger. Pal Gam, the part of Surat where my father grew up, was small too: narrow streets, houses packed close together, everyone knowing everyone else's business.

He was the son of a farmer and a housewife. Neither of them had ever had the chance at an education. His father, Sukhdevbhai Patel, worked land that never made anyone rich. His mother, Nandanben Patel, ran a household with almost nothing to work with. My father's childhood, and then his teenage years, happened inside that tight space. The family was poor first. Later, once everyone had worked hard enough, they became lower middle class.

He rarely talked about those years, even with us. What he did say, when he said anything at all, was how hard it had been to pay for his own college. His parents couldn't help him. Not because they didn't want to. Neither of them understood how any of it worked, and neither of them had the money anyway. What he gave up, what he went without, whose help he leaned on: none of that ever made it into the stories he told his own children. What made it into the stories was simple. It was hard, and he did it anyway.

By the time he finished, he had two things almost nobody around him had. A law degree. And English. In Pal Gam, back then, either one alone would have been a big deal for a farmer's son. He had both.

---

He married my mother, Heena Patel, on December 10, 1998. I was born the next year, on December 9. My sister, Janvi, came on November 11, 2002. By then he had a steady job in accounting, in the same city where he'd once been too poor to imagine having any job at all. Two children, one wife, and work he could count on. He had built that life himself, out of almost nothing.

He made sure of one thing before almost anything else. His own schooling had cost him more than he ever fully explained. He wasn't going to let that happen to his children. My sister and I were sent to English medium school, not the Gujarati medium school most families on our street chose. Back then, in Surat, an English medium seat wasn't just a school admission. It said something. It told the whole street: this family had decided where its children were going, no matter what it took to get them there. He never explained the decision out loud. He didn't need to. He was the boy who taught himself English the hard way, one exam at a time, while his own parents couldn't even check his homework. He wasn't going to let us start that far behind. In a way, it was the same instinct that made him a good son. His own parents had nothing to give him but their blessing, and he never held that against them. He just made sure it stopped with him.

The street's opinion of him never changed. People called him honest, and on that street, that word wasn't handed out easily. He worked hard. Every morning and every evening, without any fuss, he prayed. It was the same devotion that had given him the name Shambu before he was even a day old. Except now the devotion was his own. Not a name someone gave him, but a habit he chose, twice a day, year after year.

That devotion, and that reputation, were only two parts of him. There was a third. For as long as anyone could remember, some part of him wanted to be somewhere else.

He was drawn to foreign countries the way some people are drawn to a certain sound, without any real explanation for it. A work trip to Hong Kong, in China, turned that daydream into a real plan. He came back from that trip changed. He'd seen, even briefly, what life outside Surat could look like. It settled something in him that had been loose for years. He wanted to build a life for his family somewhere in North America.

By the time he was 30, he had taken the IELTS exam more than once. He had applied, through every legal channel he could find, for permanent residency in both the United States and Canada. It took years. Paperwork. Fees. The kind of hope that has to be rebuilt every time it's knocked down. None of it worked out. The one chance that would have moved his family across an ocean never came.

So he stayed in Surat. He kept his job in accounting. He kept praying morning and evening to the god whose name the street had given him before he could even talk. And by every account from people who knew him then, he was genuinely happy with the life he had instead. A wife. Two children. And a name the whole street still used without thinking twice.

---

I've heard the Hong Kong story more than once. I've heard the IELTS story too. They always come together, in the same shape: proof that he tried, and proof that trying isn't always enough. Neither story ever comes with a complaint attached, not one I've ever caught anyway. That's the part I still can't explain. A man who wanted to leave that badly, who never got the chance, and who still seemed, as far as a child could tell, genuinely content.

Say his name on Moti Fali today and people still smile. *Shambu.* Not Rakesh. Never Rakesh. For the first thirty years of his life, on that street and almost everywhere else, the name fit him perfectly.

---

Moti Fali was Kadva Patel *Samaj*. Every house on it. So was the rest of Pal Gam. But the *Samaj* didn't stop at the edge of the village. It was a caste network that had connected Patel families across Gujarat for generations. It ran everything from a single wedding to its own schools and hospitals. Inside that bigger world, my father was known by far more people than just his neighbors, and they thought of him exactly the same way.

Weddings needed someone to set things up before the guests arrived. He showed up. Funerals needed someone steady enough to stand at the front and handle what nobody else wanted to deal with. He was there. Every festival that came through the street, and in that neighborhood they came often, found him already involved, never just watching from the side. When the *Samaj* needed something organized, or a disagreement settled quietly before it turned into something bigger, his name came up first.

And the picture people had of him held up. He didn't drink. He didn't smoke. He was fully vegetarian, which in that community said as much about a person's discipline as it did about their diet. He was trustworthy with money, with responsibility, with someone else's daughter at her own wedding. Across all those weddings and funerals and festivals and quiet favors, he earned an actual place in the *Samaj*. The kind most men spend a lifetime chasing and never get.

That pull went both ways, too. He was just as often the one looking for the next thing to help with. The next committee that needed a name. The next festival that could use one more person standing at the front instead of the back. Whether something the *Samaj* did was actually good for a family or not wasn't really a question he asked, out loud or otherwise. If everyone around him believed something was simply how things were done, that was reason enough for him to believe it too. He trusted the *Samaj* completely, the same way he trusted the calendar that had given him his name.

None of that came for free.

A place like that isn't earned by showing up a few times. Neither is a name people say with that much warmth. Someone pays for it, again and again, in ways the street never sees. I didn't understand that as a child, watching him get pulled away, again, to arrange someone's wedding or lead someone's *puja*. I understood it later, and even then, only partly. The man the whole *Samaj* trusted without question had been paying for that trust the entire time. None of us saw it happen. He never once mentioned it.

I still don't know everything that price included. But I know what was riding on it, because I watched it up close from the time I was old enough to notice. His pride. His place in the *Samaj*. Every bit of respect it had taken a poor farmer's son a lifetime to build. All of it stood on something he couldn't afford to stop paying. And for a long time, he didn't.
