# Part II — Where It Began

## Chapter Two: Sukhdev Papa

I know part of that price now. Not all of it. I don't think I ever will.

But I know where a piece of it went, and it isn't where I expected. It didn't come out of his own pocket. It came out of someone else's body.

---

Our house ran on one rule above every other rule. No alcohol. No cigarettes. Not for a birthday, not for a wedding, not for any excuse a guest tried to bring through the door. Nobody in our family broke it. Not my mother. Not my sister. Not me.

Only one person ever did, and he wasn't even in our house most of the time. He was my father's father. My grandfather, Sukhdevbhai Patel. I called him Sukhdev papa, the way a grandson does.

Sukhdev papa smoked. He drank too. I don't know exactly when either habit started, only that it started before I was born, and that it ran further back than him. His own father, my great-grandfather, smoked his whole life. In a village like Pal Gam, back then, that wasn't unusual. Almost no one around them could read. Nobody was telling them what a cigarette actually did to a body over sixty or seventy years of smoking it. My great-grandfather lived past ninety anyway. He also carried tuberculosis for a long stretch of that life. Sukhdev papa wasn't as lucky with the smoking. It caught up to him early enough that he needed lung surgery. He gave up cigarettes after that. He never gave up the drink.

My father watched both of those men, his grandfather and his own father, breathe through what smoking had done to them. He was educated in a way neither of them had been. He decided, somewhere in his own boyhood, that he would never touch a cigarette, and he never did. He taught the same lesson to my sister and me before we were old enough to be tempted by one. On this single point, I've never once disagreed with him. Smoking and drinking are not harmless habits. I watched what they cost this family too. Nothing in this chapter is written to defend an addiction. It's written because of what my father decided that addiction was worth punishing.

---

If this had only ever been about health, the chapter would end here. It didn't stay about health for long.

Drinking, in my father's mind, wasn't a medical problem. It was a social one.

"Nobody will marry their daughters into this house because of your drinking," he told Sukhdev papa, more than once, in front of whoever happened to be standing close enough to hear it. That was the real arithmetic. Not what the alcohol was doing to an aging man's body. What it might do, someday, to a wedding proposal. My father had spent a lifetime building a name on that street: honest, devout, trustworthy, the man every Samaj function wanted standing at the front of the room. A father with a known drinking problem didn't fit next to a name like that. So the drinking had to stop. Not for Sukhdev papa's sake. For the name's.

> परोक्षे कार्यहन्तारं प्रत्यक्षे प्रियवादिनम् ।
> वर्जयेत्तादृशं मित्रं विषकुम्भं पयोमुखम् ॥
> — *Chanakya Niti, Chapter 2, Verse 5*
>
> **Translation:** "Avoid one who speaks sweetly to your face but works against you where you can't see it. Such a person is like a pot of poison with milk poured over the top."

Chanakya wrote that line about choosing friends, not about raising children, and I won't pretend otherwise. But the image he reached for still fits what happened in our house better than almost anything else I've come across while writing this book. Milk on the surface. Something else entirely underneath. My father wasn't a stranger smiling at someone's door. He was blood. But the shape of the thing was the same. What the street saw and what happened once that door closed were never, not once, the same house.

---

By the 2010s, there were real answers to a problem like Sukhdev papa's, even in a city the size of Surat. De-addiction centers existed, some run by hospitals, some by charitable trusts. Doctors could manage withdrawal safely, with medicine instead of a rod. Alcoholics Anonymous had been meeting quietly across India for decades by then, ordinary people sitting in a circle, saying the addiction out loud instead of hiding it. My father was devoted to that Samaj his whole life. Even there, elders existed whose entire reputation rested on talking a family gently through exactly this kind of crisis. My father knew how to ask a room full of strangers for help organizing someone else's wedding. He never once asked any of them for help with this.

Instead, he and his brother, my uncle Praful Patel, decided they would handle it themselves.

There's a boundary most people never have to think about, because they never come close enough to test it: a son does not raise his hand to his father. My father crossed it. Then he crossed it again. And again, until crossing it stopped meaning anything to him at all.

> क्रोधाद्भवति सम्मोहः सम्मोहात्स्मृतिविभ्रमः ।
> स्मृतिभ्रंशाद् बुद्धिनाशो बुद्धिनाशात्प्रणश्यति ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 2, Verses 62–63*
>
> **Translation:** "From anger comes delusion. From delusion, the loss of memory. From the loss of memory, the ruin of reason. And from the ruin of reason, a person is destroyed."

I don't think my father ever felt destroyed. That's what makes the verse land differently here than it's usually meant to. Krishna is describing what a man's own anger eventually does to him, the price it extracts from the one carrying it. My father's reputation never paid that price. He walked out of our house every morning looking exactly like the man the street already believed him to be. The reason wasn't ruined in him. It moved. It landed on Sukhdev papa instead.

---

I can't tell you the first time it happened. I don't think anyone left in my family could tell you, not exactly. I'm 28 now, and there's more from those years that I've lost than I've kept. What I can tell you is that it wasn't the last time, or the worst time, or even unusual by the time I was old enough to understand what I was watching. Over the years that followed, I count more than thirty separate nights like the ones I'm about to describe. I'm not going to walk through all thirty. What follows is what happened, over and over, in different combinations, until it stopped being an incident and became something closer to routine.

When Sukhdev papa came home having had too much to drink, and passed out the way a person does after that, my father and my uncle tied him to the bed. Then they beat the soles of his feet with a rod. In winter, they threw cold water on him to force him conscious, the same water a person might use to wake someone gently, used instead like a weapon. Some nights they burned the soles of his feet with hot utensils, so that walking the next day would hurt enough to remember. They slapped him. They beat him wherever the anger happened to land. They said things to him I'm not going to write down here, because some words don't need repeating to make their point, and because a man who never smoked or drank in his own life still isn't supposed to know words like that.

None of this happened somewhere hidden from us. It happened at home, in the same rooms where we ate dinner and did our homework. The same bed frame that held him some nights was a bed one of us slept near on others. He never once sent us out of the house first, never lowered his voice on our account, never seemed to weigh, even for a second, what a child standing in a doorway at that hour might carry forward from it.

My father was in his physical prime through those years, the kind of strength a man in his thirties carries without thinking about it. Sukhdev papa was not. He was already an old man by then. Decades of farm work had worn his body down long before the drink ever touched it. That imbalance never once seemed to register with my father as a reason to stop. If anything, it was the thing that made stopping unnecessary. There was never a night Sukhdev papa could have fought back, and I don't think he ever tried to.

> माता गुरुतरा भूमेः खात् पितोच्चतरस्तथा ।
> मनः शीघ्रतरं वाताच्चिन्ता बहुतरी तृणात् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Yaksha Prashna*
>
> **Translation:** "The mother is heavier than the earth. The father stands higher than the sky. The mind moves faster than the wind. And worry outnumbers even the blades of grass."

That's Yudhishthira's answer when a yaksha asks him what, in the whole world, truly deserves to be weighed against the earth and measured against the sky. Not a king. Not a warrior. A mother. A father. It's one of the most repeated verses in the entire epic, the kind almost every Gujarati Hindu household has heard some version of, including mine. I don't know if my father ever heard this verse and thought of Sukhdev papa: tied to his own bed, struck on the soles of his feet, by the very son who was supposed to hold him higher than the sky. I know that if he did think of it, nothing changed.

By the third or fourth time, something in how he did it had changed. The anger came faster. It needed less to set it off. What began as a response to Sukhdev papa's drinking started to look, on some nights, like a habit all its own, something reached for the way another man reaches for a cigarette: quick, automatic, over almost before it started, gone completely by morning. My father had spent his whole life making sure no one could ever call him an addict of anything. I've come to believe he had one anyway. It simply wasn't the kind anyone in the Samaj would have recognized, or judged him for, because it never once showed on him. It showed on Sukhdev papa's feet.

The next morning, he got up, got dressed, and went to work like any other day. No apology. No mention of the night before, not to Sukhdev papa, not to the rest of us. I never once saw him carry any of it on his face. Whatever it cost him, if it cost him anything at all, he kept it as well hidden as everything else he carried.

---

One night stands apart from the rest, the way one night always does, even inside a pattern like this one.

The house had gone quiet by then, the particular quiet that comes after something loud has finally stopped. My sister and I found Sukhdev papa sitting alone, still in whatever position the beating had left him in, crying. Not quietly. We were young enough that we didn't have a plan, only an instinct, the same one that sends any child toward a person in pain. We went to the kitchen. We filled a glass with water. We carried it back to him together and put it in his hands.

I don't remember which of us thought of it first. I remember his hands shaking around the glass, water trembling right up to the rim without spilling. I remember his eyes, and the particular kind of tears that come from a person who has stopped expecting anyone to come. He didn't say much. I don't think there was much left in him to say.

Neither of us understood, at that age, everything that had happened to him, or why. We understood enough. We understood that the drinking wasn't right, and that what had just happened to him wasn't right either, and that somehow both of those things were true in the same house, about the same people, on the same night. A glass of water doesn't undo a beating. We knew that even then, in whatever way children know things they can't yet put into words. It was still the only thing either of us had to give him. I still can't think about his face that night without something in my chest tightening. My sister says the same.

---

I've asked myself the same questions more times than I can count, and I still don't have satisfying answers to any of them. Where was the man who had taught himself English out of a house with no money for it? Where was the son who once dreamed of building a different life for his family in North America, a dream he stayed proud of even after it never came true? Where was the devotion that woke him every morning to pray to a god whose own name the street had given him at birth? None of it was in that room. Only the rod was in that room. The rod, and the water glass, and an old man's feet.

There's a phrase you hear early if you grow up in a Hindu household the way we did: your parents are the closest thing to god you'll ever be allowed to touch. I couldn't tell you which text that phrase traces back to first, and I won't pretend I could. But the idea runs through the stories themselves, not only through what people repeat at festivals. Rama didn't have to go into exile. He chose it, without an argument, the moment he understood it was what his father wanted of him.

> तं समीक्ष्य त्ववहितं पितुर्निर्देशपालने ।
> कौसल्या बाष्पसंरुद्धा वचो धर्मिष्ठमब्रवीत् ॥
> — *Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda, Sarga 24, Shloka 1*
>
> **Translation:** "Seeing him so intent on carrying out his father's command, Kausalya, her voice choked with tears, spoke these righteous words."

Kausalya weeps in that verse because her son is about to lose a kingdom for his father's sake, and he doesn't hesitate for a moment. Sukhdev papa wept in our house because his son had just finished hurting him for the sake of a kingdom far smaller than Ayodhya: a street's good opinion, a Samaj's approval, a future daughter-in-law's family deciding we were respectable enough to marry into. Both sons believed they were doing exactly what dharma required of them. Only one of them was right.

---

Sukhdev papa died on an ordinary night, the way most people probably do. I was the one with him when it started. He woke me, told me something was wrong in his chest, and I remember exactly how fast I moved after that, running through the house to wake everyone else, certain that if I was quick enough it would matter. It didn't. He had a heart attack, and he was gone before morning.

I still think about whether there was more I could have done in those few minutes. I've turned that night over enough times that I doubt I'll ever stop. But there's a second thought that sits right next to the first one, uglier and harder to admit to: some part of me has always believed his death spared him something worse. If he had lived long enough, and kept drinking long enough, I don't know that my father would have stopped on his own. I don't know that there was a version of that story that didn't end with somebody's hands finally going too far. I would rather carry the guilt of not saving him than carry the alternative. I think about that more than I'd like to.

---

Sukhdev papa's death didn't end anything for my sister and me. If anything, that's when the real weight of those years settled in, because by then we were old enough to understand exactly what we'd watched, and to understand that the man who'd done it was still the same man raising us.

We were both afraid of him after that. Not the ordinary kind of afraid a child feels before a scolding. The kind that changes how you stand in a room, how quickly you agree, how carefully you choose which parts of your own day are safe to mention at dinner. He knew it. I don't think there was ever a single day he didn't know it, and he never did anything to unmake it. If anything, he used it. Fear runs a household very efficiently, if you don't mind what it costs the people living inside it. It wasn't only the two of us who felt it, either. My mother did too, in her own way, though her story belongs to a different chapter, not this one.

Ask anyone on Moti Fali to describe my father and you'll hear the same three or four words every time: honest, hardworking, devoted, trustworthy. Ask my sister or me, and you'll hear different words. Fearful. Angry. Not because we misunderstood him. Because we were there for the parts the street never was.
