# Chapter Seven: The House

A silence that is broken leaves a question behind it. Why now?

I know someone will ask it. I can almost hear the words. "Ferin, why are you bringing all this up now? These are old stories. Everyone makes mistakes. Let it go. Move on."

It is a fair question, and I want to answer it honestly.

Some of these stories are old. They happened five or ten years ago. Some happened three to five years ago. Some happened only a year or two back. And a few happened last month.

So this is not a man looking back at a past he has left behind. It is a man still living in it.

I could move on if he had changed. I could move on if, even once, he had felt sorry for what he did. A person who feels guilty about his past is already a different person from the one who did it. I would have written a different book about that man, or no book at all.

That is not the case here. As I write this, in November 2026, the behaviour is the same. The mindset is the same. The ego is the same. The Samaj is the same. He still values the Samaj more than his own children. In his eyes, the Samaj still comes first, and we come after it.

Anyone who wants proof does not need to look into the past. They only need to look at the newest thing my father built.

---

He built a house.

In January 2026, the construction was finished. My father and my uncle Praful built a completely new house, from the ground up. It stands on the same spot where our old house stood, on Moti Fali, in Pal Gam.

The old house needed more than repairs. Repairs would not have been enough. So it was knocked down, all of it, and they started again from nothing. I will come back to that.

For the new house, they did not hold back on money. They spent well, and they built something beautiful. It has expensive furniture. It has chandeliers. It has spacious rooms. It has an elevator. It has solar panels on the roof, a garage for the car, and a well-kept garden at the back.

People still come to our house just to look at it. That is how fine it is.

I want to say this clearly, the way I said it in the first chapter. Everyone in our family is proud of that house. I am proud of it too. It is the biggest achievement of my father's life, and of my uncle's. In the first chapter, I wrote about a farmer's son on Moti Fali who had nobody to help him pay for college. That boy grew up and built a house that people come to see, on the same street, on the same ground. Very few people make a journey like that. He should be proud of it.

But pride does not stay pride for long, in a man who believes he has never been wrong. And my father has never once believed he was wrong. To him, he was a father, and whatever a father does for his children is right.

---

What comes with pride is ego. Slowly, over the months, his pride in the house became his ego.

He began to talk as if he alone had made it. It was his house. Everyone else in the family seemed to just happen to live there. It began to sound as if the house was more for him, and less for the rest of us.

To him, the house was not only a home for his family. It was a new position in the Samaj. More people were noticing him now. More people recognised his name, and what he was worth. He began to believe he was a more important part of the Samaj than before. And a more important man, in his mind, had more to lose. One wrong step from anyone in the family could now do more damage.

I have tried to understand why a house did this to him. I think there are a few reasons, and every one of them goes back to something in this book.

The first is that a house can be seen. In the fifth chapter, I wrote that the Samaj watched his passport fill with stamps on Facebook. A photo on a screen is gone in a day. A house stays where everyone can see it, every morning, on the street they all walk down. For a man who has lived his whole life for his image, a house is the biggest image of all.

The second is the road he travelled. He was born poor on that street. Now people come to that same street to look at what he built. That distance is real, and it is his. But a man who has come that far can begin to believe the distance itself proves he was right. Right about everything. Right about how to treat his father, his wife, and his children.

The third is the way our Samaj keeps score. In the fourth and sixth chapters, I wrote that our Samaj measures a family by its money, and by its land. By that measure, the new house made my father richer in more than money. It made him bigger in the one currency he has always cared about.

And the fourth is fear. The higher a man climbs in the Samaj's eyes, the further he can fall. So the bigger his name became, the more tightly he had to guard it. The house did not loosen his grip on us. It gave him more reason to hold on.

> आढ्योऽभिजनवानस्मि कोऽन्योऽस्ति सदृशो मया ।
> यक्ष्ये दास्यामि मोदिष्य इत्यज्ञानविमोहिताः ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 16, Verse 15*
>
> **Translation:** "'I am wealthy. I come from a great family. Who else is there like me? I will offer sacrifices. I will give gifts. I will celebrate.' This is how they speak, deluded by ignorance."

In the third chapter, I quoted this same chapter of the Gita. Krishna named the three gates of hell. Desire. Anger. Greed. A few verses before those gates, he describes the voice of the person who is walking toward them. That voice is this verse.

I am not calling my father a demon. Krishna is describing a nature, not a man. But I recognise the voice. Two verses earlier, it says, "This I have gained today. This I will gain next." Here it says, "Who else is there like me?" And it plans its prayers and its gifts in the same breath as its pride.

Two verses later, Krishna adds one more word for such people. *Mada.* It means intoxication. Krishna says they are drunk on wealth and on pride.

My father has never touched alcohol in his life. In the second chapter, I wrote that he beat his own father for drinking. He did it for the sake of his name in the Samaj. The Gita knows another way to be drunk. It does not need a bottle.

---

That pride did not stay inside his own head. He soon used it on my sister.

He began to say a new thing to her. "Why would I marry you now into anyone lower than us? I built this big house. I built our status in the Samaj. You will marry into an even bigger family than ours."

In the last chapter, I wrote that he had one test for every boy. The boy's family had to be wealthier than ours. That test was already hard to pass. Then he built the house. The test stayed the same, but the word "ours" grew bigger. Every room he added raised the bar for his daughter. Every chandelier pushed it a little higher.

I keep coming back to what he told her in the last chapter. "You have no options left." He said it as if it were her fault. But the options were not running out on their own. He was removing them himself, one brick at a time. The bigger his house became, the fewer families were left above it. In the end, he was building a wall around his own daughter and calling it a house.

Something else changed too. In the last chapter, he said Jash's family did not have enough land. Now he had a new measure. Before, it was the boy's land. Now, it was his own house. Either way, the thing being measured was never the boy, and it was never Janvi.

Janvi will soon be a doctor. She won her seat by her own work. She sat NEET twice. She survived a drop year that nearly broke her. None of that came into his sum. The house did. A house is measured in rooms and in square feet. My father had started to measure his daughter the same way. In the last chapter, he called her an earning cow. Now she was a figure to be matched to a building.

This is what I have learned from watching him. A man can have endless money. He can build the finest house on his street. But money cannot change the way a man thinks, if he does not want it to. The house got bigger. His thinking stayed exactly the size it always was.

And through all of it, he was never wrong. Not once, in his own eyes.

---

Someone might think that a man who spent so freely on a house would be generous in other ways too. They might expect that the whole family was asked how the house should be built. That is what a home is supposed to be. Everyone who lives in it has a say.

It was not like that. It was never going to be like that.

I have written about this all through the book. My father never gives anyone else an option. He never gives weight to anyone in the family except his brother. To him, everyone else's words are useless. So he decides. In the fourth chapter, I wrote that he and my uncle decided what everyone ate at restaurants. I wrote that he chose my clothes. In the last chapter, he chose Janvi's dress and her hair. He tried to choose who both of his children would marry.

The house was one more case, and a recent one. It shows that in all these years, nothing about him has changed.

Everything in the new house was chosen from scratch. The design of every room. The floor tiles. The kitchen layout. The kitchen equipment. The lights. The furniture. All of it was decided by two people. My father, and his brother.

Nobody else was asked. Not my mother. Not my Kaki. Not one suggestion from either of them was heard. As the two men saw it, women know nothing about these things. And anyway, they said, we are the ones paying for the house, so their opinion does not matter.

I want to stay with the kitchen for a moment.

In most families, the kitchen is the one room the women choose. Even in homes where the men decide everything else, this one room is theirs. It is only common sense. They are the ones who spend the most time in it. In our house, the women were not even invited. They were not taken along to see anything. They were not shown a single design. Not the rooms. Not the kitchen equipment. Nothing.

Women know nothing about these things? My mother has run a kitchen every day since she married my father in 1998. In the third chapter, I wrote that she and my Kaki were up before anyone else and in the kitchen after everyone had gone to bed. Nobody in our family knows more about a kitchen than those two women. They were the only real experts in the house. They were the only ones not asked.

And "we are paying"? In the third chapter, I wrote that for years this whole household ran on my mother's inheritance, while my father kept his own salary. Nobody gave her a voice then, either. When she paid, her money did not count. When he paid, only his money counted. Somehow, either way, the woman's voice was never needed.

> पूषा त्वेतो नयतु हस्तगृह्याश्विना त्वा प्र वहतां रथेन ।
> गृहान्गच्छ गृहपत्नी यथासो वशिनी त्वं विदथमा वदासि ॥
> — *Rigveda, Mandala 10, Sukta 85, Verse 26*
>
> **Translation:** "May Pushan lead you from here, holding your hand. May the Ashvins carry you in their chariot. Go to the house, so that you may be the mistress of the house. As one who is in command, you shall speak to the gathering."

This is from the same wedding hymn I quoted in the third chapter. There, the bride was told she would be the queen of her new household. This verse comes earlier in the same hymn. The bride is being sent off to her husband's house. And the hymn tells her what she will be there. *Grihapatni.* The mistress of the house.

Scholars read the last words in two ways. Some say she will give the orders in her household. Others say she will speak in the gathering, where decisions are made. Either way, the point is the same. She speaks. Her word counts.

That is one of the oldest things our tradition says about a woman in a house. It is spoken over her at her own wedding. My mother and my Kaki were never once asked to speak. In the old house, they were the servants of the house. In the new one, nothing changed. They were still treated as the house's maids.

---

The plainest proof of that is a machine the house does not have.

They spent so much on the house. They even put in an elevator, for convenience. But they did not buy a dishwasher for the kitchen.

I asked my uncle why.

"We already have someone for the dishes," he said.

He was pointing at the women of the house.

This is not about a dishwasher. A family can live well without one. Millions do. And it is not about money. A house with an elevator in it could have afforded a dishwasher many times over. The question was never what it cost. The question was whose time was worth saving.

The house has a machine to carry people up and down the stairs, so that nobody has to tire their legs. It has no machine to wash the plates, because the plates already had someone. Two women. My mother has washed them by hand since 1998. My Kaki has washed them for as long as I can remember. They will go on washing them, in a new kitchen that two men chose for them.

In the third chapter, I wrote that even a maid is paid for this work. She gets her money at the end of every month. She gets a day off. My uncle did not even see the women as maids. A maid is a person you have to pay. He saw them as something closer to equipment. Why buy a machine for a job that is already being done for free?

That one sentence shows a man's whole character. It shows what he thinks of women in general, and how they are treated in his own house. My father thinks the same. I wrote that in the third chapter, and the new house only proved it again.

---

I have used the word "house" all through this chapter. That was not a mistake. It was a choice.

In Gujarati, we have two words for it. A *makaan* is a building. Walls, a roof, and rooms. A *ghar* is a home. It is the place where the people inside are safe, and heard, and loved.

My father built a *makaan*. A very fine one. For me, even today, it is still a house. And for the two women who wash its plates, it is still a house, not a home.

> न गृहं गृहमित्याहुर्गृहिणी गृहमुच्यते ।
> गृहं तु गृहिणीहीनमरण्यसदृशं मतम् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, the pigeon's lament*
>
> **Translation:** "A house is not what makes a home. It is the woman of the house who is called the home. A house without her is thought of as no better than a forest."

This verse comes from a story I have told before. In the third chapter, I wrote about the pigeon who sat in a tree, crying for his wife who had not come home. "There is no refuge like a wife," he said. These lines come from the same lament, in the popular text of the Mahabharata.

The pigeon understood something my father never has. A home is not made of walls. It is made of the people inside it, and of how they are treated. By that old verse, the women of our family are the home itself. My mother is the home. My Kaki is the home. And in our new house, the home itself was told to stay out of the planning, and to keep washing the plates.

So when I say that our family's women are treated like maids in that house, I am saying something worse than it sounds. By the Mahabharata's own words, my father and my uncle are treating the home itself as its servant.

Now I can come back to the old house.

I wrote at the start of this chapter that the old house needed more than repairs, so it was knocked down. Most of this book happened inside those old walls. My grandfather was beaten there. My mother was beaten there. My sister and I grew up afraid there. Those walls are gone now. The new ones are beautiful.

But walls were never the problem. What needed more than repairs was never the building. It was the way the people inside it were treated. That did not come down with the old walls. It moved into the new house with the furniture.

A building can be knocked down and rebuilt by anyone with enough money. A mindset cannot. Only the person who carries it can tear it down and start again. My father is the only one who can do that work. Nobody can do it for him.

---

So what comes next?

To be honest, I don't know.

There is one thing I do know. My father will not change, not even a little, after reading this book. I am completely sure of that. I say it the way I would say that the sun will rise tomorrow. I have known him all my life.

I also know what he will say. He will blame me. He will say I have spoiled his image in the Samaj.

I know the exact words, because I have heard them before. In the second chapter, I wrote down what he told his own father. "You are ruining my image. Our image, in front of the Samaj." He said it to his father. One day soon, I think, he will say it to his son.

But a book cannot ruin a man's image. Only the man can do that. I did not do any of the things in these pages. I only wrote them down. If the truth spoils an image, then the image was never true in the first place.

I did not write this book to change him. I did not write it in the hope of making things better at home.

I wrote it for the people around him. For the Samaj, and for society. I wrote it to show them both sides of the coin. In the last chapter, I called it a balance sheet. It is the same idea. All their lives, people have been shown one side of my father. This book shows them the other.

> अथ यदि ते कर्मविचिकित्सा वा वृत्तविचिकित्सा वा स्यात् ।
> ये तत्र ब्राह्मणाः संमर्शिनः । युक्ता आयुक्ताः । अलूक्षा धर्मकामाः स्युः ।
> यथा ते तत्र वर्तेरन् । तथा तत्र वर्तेथाः ॥
> — *Taittiriya Upanishad, Shiksha Valli, Anuvaka 11, Verse 4*
>
> **Translation:** "Now, if you are ever in doubt about what to do, or in doubt about how to live, look to the wise people around you. Those who judge with care. Those who are devoted. Those who are not harsh. Those who love dharma. As they would act in that place, so should you act."

This book takes its name from this teaching. I have come back to it again and again. In the first chapter, I wrote that every child in a house like ours grows up hearing *Pitru devo bhava*. Treat your father as a god. In the fourth chapter, I quoted the next lines. Follow only his blameless deeds. Not the rest. In the last chapter, I showed that the teacher begins with something else. Speak the truth.

This is the line that comes right after "not the rest." The teacher seems to know what his student is going to ask next. If I cannot follow my father in everything, then whom do I follow? And the Upanishad answers. Look to the good people around you. It describes them by four marks. They judge with care. They are devoted. They are not harsh. They love dharma.

My father is devoted. I have never said otherwise. He prays every morning and every evening. But devotion is only one of the four marks. The Upanishad also asks for a person who is not harsh. He never passed that one.

More than two thousand years ago, this teaching already planned for a father like mine. It did not tell the son to obey anyway. It told him where else to look.

I know where I looked. I looked at my mother, who stopped his hand when he raised it against my sister. I looked at the father in our own society who stood in front of everyone and said, "I will take care of my daughter's future." I looked at Savitri's father in the Mahabharata, who let his daughter choose. They are not harsh. They judge with care. That is where I learned what a father can be.

---

So I will say it plainly, one last time. This is why I wrote this book.

I wrote it for the Samaj. For years, the Samaj has looked at my father and seen an ideal man. I am not asking anyone to stop respecting him. I am asking them to look at the whole man before they decide. A man is not measured by the weddings he arranges, or by the house he builds, or by how kindly he speaks to other people's wives. In the third chapter, I wrote that a house measures a man by the woman in his own kitchen. I am asking the Samaj to measure him there too. And to measure the rest of us there. Land, money, and the size of a house have become the Samaj's measure of a family. That measure is breaking its own daughters.

I wrote it for fathers like mine. There are many of them. Educated men. Travelled men. Men who pray. Their children are not their image. They are not an investment, and they are not a rank in the Samaj. They are people. If one father reads this and puts the phone down before he shouts at his daughter, this book has done more than I hoped.

I wrote it for the sons and daughters who live in houses like ours. I want them to know they are not the only ones. Respect is not the same as silence. The Upanishad itself says to follow only the blameless deeds. Not the rest. Choosing is not a sin. It is what the teaching asks.

I wrote it for two women who have never been written about. My mother and my Kaki. For as long as I can remember, their work in that house has been invisible. Nobody thanked them. Nobody asked them. This book is the first place their work has been written down where everyone can see it.

I wrote it for my sister, who wanted her voice heard. This is her voice.

I wrote it for my grandfather, Sukhdev papa, who cannot tell his own story anymore. When we were children, my sister and I brought him a glass of water. This book is the only other thing I can bring him.

And I wrote it for myself. In the fourth chapter, I said the chain my father chose not to break would end with me. This book is how I break it. Some day I will build a home of my own. In that home, everyone will have a say. The people who use the kitchen will choose the kitchen. And nobody will be "someone for the dishes."

---

This is where the story stands, at the end of the book. It has no ending yet.

My father still prays every morning and every evening, now in the new house. My mother and my Kaki are still in the kitchen, washing the plates by hand. Janvi will soon be a doctor. She is still fighting for the right to choose her own life, and our mother is still standing beside her. I am still on the phone, from the other side of the world.

I do not know how the story will end. I do not know whom my sister will marry. I do not know what my father will say when he reads this. I do not know whether he will ever say sorry, to anyone.

I only know that the truth is now on the page, both sides of it. I set it down as carefully as I could. I did not make anything bigger than it was. I gave him credit wherever it was owed. Whoever reads it is free to do the sum for themselves.

Nobody on Moti Fali called my father by his name. That was the first line of this book. They called him Shambu, after the god he prays to. They still do.

People still come to Moti Fali to see his house. They see the chandeliers, the elevator, and the garden. They leave thinking they have seen it all. Now they have read what the walls never showed them.

It is a beautiful house.

It is still not a home.
