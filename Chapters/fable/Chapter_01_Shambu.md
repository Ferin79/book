# Chapter One: Shambu

Nobody on Moti Fali called my father by his name.

Moti Fali is the street where he was born. It runs through Pal Gam, which in those days was a village beside the city of Surat. He grew up on that street. He raised his own children on it. And everyone who lived there knew him by a name his parents never gave him.

"Shambu, kem che?" a neighbour would call out as he passed.

He would lift a hand and keep walking. He always had somewhere to be. Uncles called him Shambu. Aunties who had known him since he was a baby called him Shambu. The boys he grew up with still called him that when they were grown men, without a second thought.

His real name is Rakesh Patel. The street gave him the other one, and it was not a random choice. He was born on a Monday, August 2, 1976. That Monday was the first Monday of Shravan.

Anyone raised in a Hindu home in Gujarat knows why that matters. Shravan is the month of Shiva. It comes with the rains, and for anyone who worships him it is the holiest month of the year. Every Monday in it is his day. Families fast. People walk to the temple before the day's work begins. They wait all year for the first of those Mondays to come around again.

A boy born on that day does not only get a name. He gets claimed. Shambhu is one of Shiva's own names. The street took it up the way a nickname sticks to a child, long before anyone knows whether it will suit him.

It suited him.

A street does not keep a nickname alive for a whole lifetime out of habit alone. It keeps the name because it likes the man. Moti Fali liked my father. This chapter is about why.

---

The reasons begin with where he came from.

Surat is a city built on diamonds and cloth. It is full of people who came from somewhere small and worked their way toward something bigger. Pal Gam was one of the small places. It had narrow streets and houses packed close together, and everyone knew everyone else's business. Beyond the houses lay the fields.

My father's father worked those fields. His name was Sukhdevbhai Patel, and he was a farmer. The land fed the family. It never made anyone rich. My father's mother, Nandanben Patel, ran the house with almost nothing to run it on. Neither of them ever had the chance to study. A boy in that house who wanted an education had nobody at home to ask.

My father's childhood happened inside that tight space. So did his teenage years. The family was poor first. Later, after years of hard work, it became lower middle class. That was as far as a small farm could carry it.

He rarely spoke about those years, even to us. When he did, he spoke about one thing. He spoke about how hard it had been to pay for his own college. His parents could not help him. It was not that they didn't want to. They had no money to give, and they did not understand how any of it worked. The forms. The fees. The admissions. He had to find his own way through all of it.

How he did it, I cannot say. He never told us what he gave up, or what he went without, or whose help he took. I will not guess. This book holds only what I saw and what I was told. What he told us was short. It was hard, and he did it anyway.

When he finished, he had two things almost nobody around him had. A law degree. And English. In Pal Gam, in those years, either one would have been a big thing for a farmer's son. He had both. He had learned English the slow way, one exam at a time, in a house where nobody could check his homework.

That is the first true thing about my father, and I want it written down before anything else. Nobody handed him anything. Whatever he had, he built.

---

Next, he built a family.

He married my mother, Heena Patel, on December 10, 1998. He was 22. I was born the next year, on December 9, one day before their first anniversary. My sister, Janvi, came on November 11, 2002.

By then he had a steady job in accounting, in the same city where he had once been too poor to picture having any job at all. It is careful work. Every figure has to agree with every other figure, and nothing is allowed to go missing. He was good at it.

He was 26 years old. He had a degree, a job, a wife, a son, and a daughter. He had made that life himself, out of almost nothing.

We lived as a joint family. My grandparents, my parents, my father's brother Praful and, in time, his wife and children. One roof. One kitchen. That house on Moti Fali is where most of this book takes place.

Before almost anything else, my father settled one question. His own schooling had cost him more than he ever explained. He was not going to let that happen to his children. So my sister and I were sent to an English medium school. Most families on our street chose the Gujarati medium one.

In Surat, in those years, an English medium seat was more than a school admission. It was a statement. It told the whole street that this family had decided where its children were going. He never explained the decision out loud. He didn't need to. He was the boy who had learned English alone, the hard way. His children were not going to start that far behind.

There was no bitterness in it. His parents had nothing to give him except their blessing, and he never held that against them. He only made sure the struggle ended with him.

---

The street watched all of this. Its opinion of him only ever went up.

People called him honest. On Moti Fali, that word was not handed out easily. People called him hardworking, and he was. And every morning and every evening, without any fuss, he folded his hands and prayed to Shiva.

That is where the name came back around. The street had called him Shambu before he was a day old, because of a date on a calendar. He grew up and made the name true. The devotion was his own now. Nobody gave it to him. He chose it twice a day, year after year.

Ask anyone on Moti Fali to describe my father, and the same words come back every time. Honest. Hardworking. Devoted. Trustworthy. I have heard those four words about him all my life.

On that street, I was Shambu's son before I was anything else. My sister was Shambu's daughter. It was a good thing to be. People smiled when they heard his name. They still do.

I want to be fair about this, because the rest of the book depends on it. None of what the street saw was false. The honesty was real. The hard work was real. The prayers were real, and I watched them every day. Whatever else these pages say about my father, they will not take those things away from him.

---

There was one more side to him, and the street knew this one too. Some part of him always wanted to be somewhere else.

He was drawn to foreign countries. I cannot fully explain why. Some people are simply made that way. For years it was only a daydream. Then his work sent him on a trip to Hong Kong, and the daydream became a plan. He came back changed. He had seen, even for a short while, what life outside Surat could look like. He wanted to build a life for his family in North America.

Around the age of 30, he sat the IELTS exam more than once. It is the English test that countries like Canada ask for. He applied for permanent residency in the United States and in Canada, through every legal channel he could find. It took years. Paperwork. Fees. Hope that had to be rebuilt each time it was knocked down.

None of it worked out. The one chance that would have carried his family across an ocean never came.

So he stayed. He kept his job in accounting. He kept praying, morning and evening. And by every account from the people who knew him then, he was content with the life he had.

I have heard the Hong Kong story more than once. I have heard the IELTS story too. They always arrive together, and they always have the same shape. He tried. Trying was not enough. Neither story ever came with a complaint, or not one that I caught.

The dream itself did not die. It waited. Years later it found someone else to carry it across that ocean, and that someone was me. But that belongs to a later chapter.

---

So far, I have written about one street. My father's name travelled much further than that. It travelled through one word. Samaj.

The word simply means community. For us, it means something exact. We are Kadva Patels, and the Kadva Patel Samaj is what ties our families together across Gujarat. It holds the festivals and the gatherings. It does charitable work, and it has built schools and hospitals of its own. It also helps families find brides and grooms for their children inside the community.

Every house on Moti Fali belonged to it. So did the rest of Pal Gam. In a village like that, the Samaj is not something a family joins. A family is born inside it. It is where a family's name is kept.

And the name matters more than an outsider might think. When a family looks for a bride or a groom, the first thing it asks about is the other family's name. A good name is not only a matter of pride. It decides who a family's children will marry.

Inside that wider world, far more people knew my father than his own neighbours. They thought of him exactly the way the street did.

A wedding needed someone to set things up before the guests came. He was there. A funeral needed someone steady to stand at the front and handle what nobody else wanted to handle. He was there too. Festivals came through our neighbourhood often, and each one found him already at work, never watching from the side. When the Samaj needed something organised, or a quarrel settled quietly before it grew, his name came up first.

I watched him leave the house for those functions more times than I can count. A wedding. A funeral. A *puja* at someone's home. I did not think about it then. Children don't.

And the man the Samaj saw held up under any look. He did not drink. He did not smoke. He was fully vegetarian, which in our community says as much about a man's discipline as about his plate. He could be trusted with money. He could be trusted with responsibility. A family could hand him the arrangements for a daughter's wedding and stop worrying.

Wedding by wedding, funeral by funeral, favour by favour, he earned a real place in the Samaj. It is the kind of place most men chase all their lives and never get.

He did not inherit it. Before him, nobody in our family had any such standing. His father had spent his life bent over a field, not in a wedding hall or a committee meeting. A farmer does not build a name like that. My father built it from nothing, the same way he had built the degree, the job, and the family.

He never eased off, either. Some men his age had family money or a family business to lean on, and they did not push themselves very hard. He had no such cushion. He took a salaried job, and he showed up for it the way he showed up for every wedding and every funeral. Every single time, without being asked twice.

"Look at Shambu," people would say. "That is what a son should be."

The pull went both ways. The Samaj called on him, and he went looking for the Samaj. The next committee that needed a name. The next festival that could use one more man at the front. He never seemed to ask whether a custom was actually good for a family. If everyone around him treated something as the way things were done, that was reason enough for him. He trusted the Samaj completely, the same way he trusted the calendar that had given him his name.

---

That was my father, as the world knew him. A farmer's son who built everything he had. Honest. Devout. Trusted. The man a whole community pointed to.

Everything in this chapter is true. I have not made any of it bigger than it was, and I have not held any of it back. I needed all of it on the page first, because of what comes next. It would be easy to tell a story about a bad man. This is not that story. This is the story of a man a whole street admired, and of what it took to keep that admiration.

There is a line that every child in a house like ours grows up hearing. It is far older than the street, and far older than the Samaj. *Pitru devo bhava.* Treat your father as a god.

I knew that line long before I could have explained it. In the first years of my life, it asked nothing hard of me. I only had to look at the father I had been given. If any father had earned that line, it seemed to be mine.

The line has more words after it. Nobody taught me those. I found them much later, on my own, and I will come to them when it is time.

First, there is something I did not understand as a child. None of what my father had came for free.

A name like his is not earned once and then owned. It has to be kept. It has to be kept every day, in front of everyone, without a single crack. One story told at the wrong function can undo years of weddings and funerals. He knew that better than anyone. I think it was the thing he feared most.

Keeping a name like that has a cost. My father kept accounts for a living. He knew that every entry has two sides. For every credit, there is a debit somewhere. The street saw his credits, and they made a long, clean column. Honest. Hardworking. Devoted. Ideal.

The street never asked where the other column was kept.

It was kept in our house. The street saw my father's hands folded in prayer. It saw them busy at every wedding and every funeral. What those hands did behind our own door, it never saw. I grew up behind that door. I know what the name cost.

My father did not pay for it himself. Other people did. The first one I watched pay was an old man.
