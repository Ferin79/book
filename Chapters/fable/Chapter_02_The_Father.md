# Chapter Two: The Father

The old man was his father.

His name was Sukhdevbhai Patel. I called him Sukhdev papa, the way a grandson does. He was the farmer from the last chapter, the man who worked the fields around Pal Gam and never grew rich from them. He lived under the same roof as the rest of us.

I ended the last chapter with a line every child in a house like ours grows up hearing. Treat your father as a god. My father grew up hearing it too. He was a son long before he was a father.

So this chapter is not about my father as a father. It is about my father as a son. It belongs to Sukhdev papa, because he is the one who paid.

I know part of that price now. Not all of it. I don't think I ever will. But I know where the first piece of it went. It did not come out of my father's pocket. It came out of his father's body.

---

It began with a rule.

Our house ran on one rule above every other. No alcohol. No cigarettes. Not for a birthday. Not for a wedding. Not for any reason a guest might carry to our door. My mother kept that rule. My sister kept it. I kept it, and I still do.

Only one person in the house ever broke it. Sukhdev papa smoked, and he drank.

I don't know when either habit began. I only know that both began before I was born, and that they ran back further than him. His own father, my great-grandfather, smoked his whole life. In a village like Pal Gam, in those days, that was ordinary. Almost nobody around them could read. Nobody was telling them what a cigarette does to a body over sixty or seventy years.

My great-grandfather lived past ninety anyway. He also carried tuberculosis for a long part of that life. Sukhdev papa was not as lucky. The smoking caught up with him early, and he needed surgery on his lungs. After that, he gave up cigarettes. He never gave up the drink.

My father grew up watching both of those men. He watched his grandfather and his father fight for breath. He was educated in a way neither of them had been. Somewhere in his boyhood he decided that he would never touch a cigarette or a drink, and he never did. He taught the same lesson to my sister and me before we were old enough to be tempted.

On this one point, I have never disagreed with him. Smoking and drinking are not harmless habits. I saw what they cost this family. Nothing in this chapter is written to defend an addiction.

It is written because of what my father decided that addiction deserved.

---

If the rule had only been about health, this chapter would end here. It did not stay about health for long.

In my father's mind, his father's drinking was not a medical problem. It was a problem of reputation.

"Nobody will marry their daughters into this house because of your drinking," he told Sukhdev papa. He said it more than once.

He also put it more plainly. "You are ruining my image," he told him. "Our image, in front of the Samaj."

That was the real sum, and he said it himself. His image came first. The family's came second. He was not counting what the alcohol was doing to an old man's body. He was counting what it might do to his name, and, one day, to a marriage proposal.

In the last chapter, I wrote that the Samaj is where a family's name is kept, and that the name decides who a family's children will marry. My father had spent his whole life building that name. Honest. Devout. Trustworthy. The man every function wanted at the front of the room. A father with a known drinking habit did not fit beside a name like that.

So the drinking had to stop. Not for Sukhdev papa's sake. For the name's.

Fear sat underneath all of it. It was not the fear that his father would come to harm. It was the fear of what the Samaj would say if it ever found out. My father knew exactly how a story travels on a street like Moti Fali. One neighbour smells it. One relative repeats it at the wrong function. After that, it is no longer one family's private trouble. It is a story the Samaj tells about that family.

I had watched him build his life around that fear long before I understood what I was watching. He never turned down a committee. He never sat out a festival. And he lived by a rule he never once said out loud. What happened inside our house mattered less than what the street believed about it.

Sukhdev papa's drinking broke that rule. It was a failure my father could not control, and it lived inside his own walls. That frightened him more than almost anything outside them could. So he did not try to heal the man. He tried to erase the problem, before anyone beyond our door had the chance to talk.

> परोक्षे कार्यहन्तारं प्रत्यक्षे प्रियवादिनम् ।
> वर्जयेत्तादृशं मित्रं विषकुम्भं पयोमुखम् ॥
> — *Chanakya Niti, Chapter 2, Verse 5*
>
> **Translation:** "Stay away from the friend who speaks sweetly to your face and ruins your work behind your back. He is a pot of poison with milk on top."

Chanakya wrote that line about choosing friends, not about fathers and sons. I will not pretend otherwise. But the picture he reached for fits our house better than almost anything else I have found. Milk on the surface. Something else underneath. The rule against drinking looked like care for a family's health. Underneath, it was care for a name. What the street saw and what happened once our door closed were never the same house.

---

Even behind that closed door, there was a right way to deal with a father who drank. A problem like Sukhdev papa's did not need to be hidden. It could have been treated.

By the 2010s, even a city like Surat had real answers for a man who could not stop drinking. De-addiction centres existed. Some were run by hospitals, and some by charitable trusts. Doctors could bring a man off alcohol safely, with medicine. Alcoholics Anonymous had been meeting quietly across India for decades. Ordinary people sat in a circle and said the addiction out loud, so that nobody had to carry it alone. And inside the Samaj itself, there were elders whose whole standing rested on guiding a family gently through trouble like this.

My father knew how to ask for help. He asked rooms full of people for help with other families' weddings. He never once asked anyone for help with this. Asking would have meant telling.

Instead, he and his brother, my uncle Praful Patel, decided to handle it themselves.

> अक्रोधेन जयेत्क्रोधमसाधुं साधुना जयेत् ।
> जयेत्कदर्यं दानेन जयेत्सत्येन चानृतम् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Vidura Niti), Chapter 39, Verse 58*
>
> **Translation:** "Conquer anger with calm. Conquer the bad with good. Conquer the miser with giving. Conquer the lie with truth."

Vidura says this to King Dhritarashtra. The king is blind, and on this night he cannot sleep. His own family is sliding toward a war, and he has done nothing to stop it. So he sends for Vidura, his half-brother and the wisest man in his court, and Vidura talks to him until morning. This verse comes from that long night. It is four short rules. The second one is the one I need. Conquer the bad with good.

My grandfather's drinking was bad. I have said so. By Vidura's rule, the answer to it was something good. A doctor. A centre. An elder's patient word. My father and my uncle answered it with something far worse than the drinking itself.

Two verses later, Vidura names what grows for a man who serves the old. His good name. His long life. His honour. His strength. My father wanted a good name more than anything on earth. Vidura had already told him where one grows.

He went the other way. There is a line most people never have to think about, because they never come near it. A son does not raise his hand to his father. My father crossed it. Then he crossed it again. He kept crossing it until it stopped being a line at all.

---

I cannot say when he first crossed it. I don't think anyone left in my family could. I have lost more from those years than I have kept. What I can say is that it was not rare. Over the years that followed, I remember more than thirty separate times. I am not going to walk through all thirty. What follows is what happened, again and again, in different combinations, until it stopped being an incident and became a routine.

It happened on the days Sukhdev papa drank. He would drink too much and pass out, the way a man does. Then my father and my uncle tied him to the bed.

They beat the soles of his feet with a rod. In winter, they threw cold water on him to force him awake. Sometimes they burned the soles of his feet with hot utensils, so that he could not walk properly the next day. They slapped him. They hit him wherever the anger happened to land.

And they cursed him. I mean the worst words our language has. I will not write them down. They are not the words of an educated man, and they are not the words of a religious one.

> माता गुरुतरा भूमेः पिता उच्चतरश्च खात् ।
> मनः शीघ्रतरं वायोश्चिन्ता बहुतरी नृणाम् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Aranyaka Parva (Yaksha Prashna), Chapter 297, Verse 41*
>
> **Translation:** "The mother is heavier than the earth. The father is higher than the sky. The mind is faster than the wind. And worries outnumber men."

These lines come from one of the best-known scenes in the Mahabharata. A yaksha, a spirit guarding a lake, puts riddle after riddle to Yudhishthira. One of them goes like this. What is heavier than the earth? What is higher than the sky? Yudhishthira does not name a mountain. He does not name a king. He names a mother and a father.

Higher than the sky. That is where the epic puts a father. My father put his on a bed, and tied him to it, and struck the soles of his feet.

I don't know if my father ever heard this verse and thought of Sukhdev papa. I know that if he did, nothing changed.

---

There is one detail in all of this that I keep coming back to. Sukhdev papa was passed out.

My father did not beat him while he was awake and arguing. He beat him while he lay senseless. He chose the exact moment when the old man was least able to understand what was happening to him, let alone learn anything from it.

> न वधः पूज्यते लोके सुप्तानामिह धर्मतः ।
> तथैव न्यस्तशस्त्राणां विमुक्तरथवाजिनाम् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Sauptika Parva, Chapter 5, Verse 9*
>
> **Translation:** "In this world, dharma gives no honour to the killing of men who are asleep. It is the same for those who have put down their weapons, and for those who have let go of their chariots and horses."

This comes from the night after the great war ended. Ashvatthama is wild with rage, and he plans to attack the enemy camp while it sleeps. His uncle, Kripa, tries to stop him with this verse. The men in that camp are his enemies. They have killed his own father. Even so, Kripa says, dharma has a rule. A warrior does not strike people who are asleep. In the next lines he describes them. Their armour is off. They are trusting the night. They lie senseless, like the dead. Whoever harms men in that state, he says, will sink into a hell with no bottom and no raft.

Kripa was talking about killing, in a war. My father did not kill his father, and I will not stretch the verse to say he did. But the rule underneath it is plain. Even an enemy is safe while he sleeps. Sukhdev papa was not an enemy. He had passed out. He could not lift a hand, or speak, or even know who was standing over him. That was the state my father chose to hit him in.

And then came the part that has never added up for me. The next day, Sukhdev papa would be sober. He could listen. He could have been spoken to. My father said nothing. He never sat him down. He never told him to stop drinking. He never brought up the night before at all, not as a warning, and not as a plea.

If the beatings were meant to teach a lesson, the lesson never reached the one man who needed it. The man who was punished could not hear it. The man who could hear it was never told a word.

It was not a plan that failed. It was never a plan at all.

---

So if it was not a plan, what was it?

I can only answer from what I saw, and I saw a great deal. None of it was hidden from us. It happened at home, in the same rooms where we ate and slept. My father never sent my sister and me out first. He never lowered his voice for our sake. He never seemed to ask himself where his children were, or what they were seeing. He was teaching us something on every one of those days. He never stopped to ask what it was.

This is what I saw. He was in the prime of his strength through those years. Sukhdev papa was not. He was an old man by then. A lifetime of farm work had worn his body down long before the drink did. That difference never seemed to reach my father as a reason to stop. If anything, it made stopping unnecessary. Sukhdev papa could never have fought back. I don't think he ever tried.

I also saw that it did not ease with time. It grew. After the first few times, something in the way he did it had changed. The anger came faster. It needed less to set it off. What began as an answer to his father's drinking started to look like a habit of its own. He had grown used to beating his own father.

My father had spent his whole life making sure no one could ever call him an addict. I have come to believe he was one anyway. His addiction was not the kind the Samaj would ever notice, or judge him for, because it never showed on him. It showed on the soles of his father's feet.

> ध्यायतो विषयान्पुंसः सङ्गस्तेषूपजायते ।
> सङ्गात्सञ्जायते कामः कामात्क्रोधोऽभिजायते ॥
> क्रोधाद्भवति सम्मोहः सम्मोहात्स्मृतिविभ्रमः ।
> स्मृतिभ्रंशाद् बुद्धिनाशो बुद्धिनाशात्प्रणश्यति ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 2, Verses 62–63*
>
> **Translation:** "When a man keeps dwelling on the things his senses want, he grows attached to them. From attachment comes craving. From craving comes anger. From anger comes delusion. From delusion, the loss of memory. From the loss of memory, the ruin of reason. And when reason is ruined, the man is lost."

Krishna is describing a staircase, and every step on it leads down. He is talking about the pull of the senses. My father's pull was different. The thing he could not stop thinking about was his own good name. But the staircase was the same. He thought about that name day and night. He grew attached to it. He craved it. And when his father's drinking stood in its way, the craving turned into anger, exactly as the verse says it will.

After that, the verse and my father part ways. Krishna says the angry man is the one who is lost in the end. My father never looked lost. He walked out of our house every morning looking like the man the street believed in. The ruin Krishna promises did come. It only landed on someone else. It landed on Sukhdev papa.

After each of those nights, my father got up the next morning, got dressed, and went to work as on any other day. There was no apology. There was no mention of the night before. I never once saw guilt on his face. In his mind, I think, it was simply something that had to be done.

---

For him, every one of those nights was over by morning. For me, one of them never ended. It stands apart from all the rest.

The house had gone quiet, the way a house does after something loud has stopped. My sister and I found Sukhdev papa sitting alone. He was crying. He was an old man, sobbing in his own house, after a beating from his own sons.

We were small. We did not have a plan. We only had the instinct that sends any child toward a person in pain. We went to the kitchen and filled a glass with water. We carried it back together and put it in his hands.

I don't remember which of us thought of it first. I remember his hands shaking around the glass. I remember his eyes. They were the eyes of a man who had stopped expecting anyone to come. He did not say much. I don't think there was much left in him to say.

Neither of us understood everything that had happened to him, or why. We understood enough. We knew the drinking was not right. We knew that what had just been done to him was not right either. Both things were true in the same house, on the same night.

A glass of water does not undo a beating. We knew that, even then, in the way children know things they cannot yet put into words. It was the only thing we had to give him. I still cannot think of his face that night without something tightening in my chest. My sister says the same.

---

I was a child that night, and I had no questions. The questions came later. They have never left.

I am grown now, and I can weigh the two wrongs in that house against each other. An old man's drinking hurt himself most of all. What his sons did about it hurt everyone who lived there. The second wrong was far heavier than the first. And it was done by the one man in the family who should have known better.

Where was the man who had taught himself English in a house with no money for it? Where was the law degree? A man who has studied the law knows what it calls tying up an old man and beating him.

Where was the dreamer? My father spent years trying to move his family to North America. In the countries he dreamed of, what he did in our house is a crime. A man who did it there would have gone to prison, and for a long time. It is a crime in India too. But a law only works when someone outside the house knows.

And where was the devotee? Where was the man who folded his hands every morning and every evening?

> पिता स्वर्गः पिता धर्मः पिता परमकं तपः ।
> पितरि प्रीतिमापन्ने सर्वाः प्रीयन्ति देवताः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Chapter 258, Verse 20*
>
> **Translation:** "The father is heaven. The father is dharma. The father is the highest penance. When the father is pleased, all the gods are pleased."

These are the words of a young man named Chirakari, in a story Bhishma tells. Chirakari has been given a terrible order by his own father, and he sits down to think before he acts. He starts by weighing what a father is. I will return to his story later in this book, because it does not end where this verse does. For now, the last line is enough. When the father is pleased, all the gods are pleased.

My father prayed to Shiva every morning and every evening. He never missed. By this verse, the surest way to please any god was already living under his roof. The verse does not ask the father to be perfect. Sukhdev papa was not. It does not ask the son to copy his father's faults, either. It only says where the gods look when a man folds his hands.

They would have looked at the soles of an old man's feet.

The stories teach the same thing as the verse. Every child in a Hindu home hears about Rama. He did not have to go into exile. He chose it, without an argument, the moment he understood that his father's word required it.

> तं समीक्ष्य त्ववहितं पितुर्निर्देशपालने ।
> कौसल्या बाष्पसंरुद्धा वचो धर्मिष्ठमब्रवीत् ॥
> — *Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda, Sarga 24, Shloka 1*
>
> **Translation:** "Seeing him so set on carrying out his father's command, Kausalya, her voice choked with tears, spoke these righteous words."

Kausalya weeps in that verse because her son is about to give up a kingdom for his father's word, and he does not hesitate. Sukhdev papa wept in our house because his son had just hurt him for the sake of a much smaller kingdom. A street's good opinion. A Samaj's approval. Some future bride's family deciding we were respectable enough to marry into.

Both sons believed they were doing what dharma asked of them. Only one of them was right.

On Moti Fali, people used to say, "Look at Shambu. That is what a son should be." The epics have their own picture of what a son should be. I have set the two side by side. I do not need to add anything.

---

The beatings did not end because my father chose to end them. They ended when Sukhdev papa died.

It was an ordinary night. I was the one with him when it began. He woke me and told me something was wrong in his chest. I remember how fast I moved after that. I ran through the house and woke everyone, sure that if I was quick enough, it would matter. It did not. It was a heart attack, and he was gone before morning.

I still ask myself whether I could have done more in those few minutes. I have turned that night over so many times that I don't expect to ever stop.

There is a second thought beside the first one. It is uglier, and harder to admit. Some part of me has always believed that his death spared him something worse. If he had lived longer, and kept drinking, I don't know that my father would ever have stopped by himself. I don't know that it would not have ended with someone's hands going too far. I would rather carry the guilt of not saving him than picture that other ending. I think about it more than I would like to.

I hope his soul is at rest. I remember him with respect, and I always will.

I could not do anything for him then. I was a child. What I can do now is write down how he lived in his own house, at the hands of his own sons. It is the only justice I have left to give him.

---

His death did not end anything for my sister and me. The real weight of those years settled on us afterwards. We were old enough by then to understand what we had watched. We were old enough to understand that the man who had done it was still the man raising us.

We were both afraid of him after that. It was not the ordinary fear a child feels before a scolding. It went deeper. It changed how we stood in a room. It changed how quickly we agreed with him. It changed which parts of our own day we thought were safe to mention at dinner.

He knew. I don't believe there was a single day he didn't know. He never did anything to undo it. He used it. Fear runs a house very efficiently, for a man who does not mind what it costs the people living in it.

That is what those years taught us. My father meant to teach one lesson in that house. Drink destroys a man. We learned a different one. We learned what he was willing to do to anyone who put his name at risk. Even an old man. Even his own father.

I still believe in the rule itself. A rule that protects a person is a good rule. But a rule that only protects a name will hurt whoever stands in its way. In our house, the first person standing in its way was the man my father was supposed to treat as a god.

In the first chapter, I wrote down the four words Moti Fali uses for my father. Honest. Hardworking. Devoted. Trustworthy. Ask my sister or me, and the words are different. A man to be feared. An angry man.

We did not misunderstand him. We were only standing where the street never stood. We were inside the house.

And we were not the only ones in there. One more person lived inside that fear. She could not grow up and move away from it, the way a child can. She was married to him.
