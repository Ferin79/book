# Chapter Three: The Wife

She was married to him.

My mother's name is Heena Patel. She became my father's wife on December 10, 1998, and she has been his wife ever since.

I ended the last chapter by saying that my sister and I were not the only ones in that house who lived in fear of him. My mother feared him too. Hers was a different kind of fear from ours. A child can grow up and go. She could not leave him, and she still had to cook his dinner every night.

This chapter is hers.

---

Her story is set where every story in this family is set. In the same house.

After Sukhdev papa died, that house was still full. My parents. My sister and me. My uncle Praful, his wife, and their two children. My grandmother. Nine of us under one roof, eating from one kitchen, the way it had been since before I was born. We had cremated Sukhdev papa, and nothing else in the house had changed. The joint family held together. The man at its centre was exactly who he had always been.

Nothing had changed for the two women who ran that kitchen, either. My mother was one of them. My uncle's wife, my Kaki, was the other.

They were up before anyone else in the house. They swept the floors and did the washing. They cooked for all nine of us, meal after meal. They served everyone else first. They ate last, after the whole house had finished. Then they washed every plate and started on the next meal.

There was no day off. If one of them was sick, the work was still expected, and she did it anyway. The men of the house never helped with any of it. They never said thank you, either. The two women even needed permission for things that should have been theirs to decide. To visit their own parents. To spend money.

To my grandmother, my uncle, and my father, these two women were the servants of the house. They were there to work. They were not supposed to expect anything back.

Even a housemaid is paid for this work. She gets her money at the end of every month, and nobody calls it a favour. She gets a day off. My mother and my Kaki did a maid's work and more, every day, for years. They never got a rupee for it. They never got a day off. Nobody in that house thought they were owed either one.

My father did more than expect it. In his mind, that was simply what women were for. They were made for work, and they were made to be under him. He did not treat them with respect. He did not care for them at all. Not his own wife. Not his brother's wife. To him, they were part of the house, like the stove or the water pot. Useful. Always there. Not something a man thanks.

---

Outside the house, he was a different man with women.

On Moti Fali, he greeted the women he passed warmly. He spoke to them softly. He never raised his voice at any of them. If a woman needed an errand run, a favour done, or a problem sorted out, he went out of his way to help her. At Samaj functions, and on visits to other people's homes, he praised women out loud for their cooking and their work.

At home, two women cooked every meal he ate, for years. I cannot remember him thanking either of them once.

It was the same man, on the same street, in the same years. Out there, he was gentle with women who were not his family. At home, he was never gentle with the two women who kept his house running. I think I understand it now. His kindness went where the Samaj could see it. Nobody from the Samaj was standing in our kitchen.

> पूज्या लालयितव्याश्च स्त्रियो नित्यं जनाधिप ।
> अपूजिताश्च यत्रैताः सर्वास्तत्राफलाः क्रियाः ।
> तदैव तत्कुलं नास्ति यदा शोचन्ति जामयः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Chapter 46, Verse 5*
>
> **Translation:** "Women must always be honoured and cherished, O king. Where they are not honoured, every sacred rite is fruitless. The moment the women of a family grieve, that family is already lost."

Bhishma says this to Yudhishthira from his bed of arrows. It comes in the middle of a teaching about marriage and the women of a family. A few lines earlier, he names the men who owe women this honour. Their fathers. Their brothers. Their fathers-in-law. And their husband's brothers. So this is not a verse about being polite to women on the street. It is about the women inside a man's own house. To my Kaki, my father was her husband's brother. To my mother, my uncle was. Bhishma's list names them both.

My father prayed every morning and every evening, year after year. He prayed in a house where the two women who ran it were not honoured. By the Mahabharata's own words, those prayers bore no fruit.

And the verse ends with a warning. A family whose women grieve is already lost. From the street, ours looked whole.

---

From the street, it looked better than whole. It looked like a family on its way up.

By every measure the street would use, the years after Sukhdev papa died were the best this family ever had. Pal Gam stopped being a village while I was still a boy. The city reached it and kept going, and one day the maps had Surat where the fields used to be. When a village becomes part of a city, everything it sits on changes price.

My family had always held land around Pal. It was the same land Sukhdev papa had bent over all his life, for very little. Suddenly it was worth more lying empty than it had ever been worth planted. Money came in that nobody had worked a harvest for. We went from lower middle class to upper middle class. We became the kind of family that owns more than it uses.

My father had grown up counting every rupee twice before he let it go. Now he didn't have to. That is the part of this chapter I still turn over the most. He was handed room to breathe. Inside his own house, nobody else got to breathe any easier.

---

There was one kind of spending he never minded. It was the kind the Samaj could see.

While Sukhdev papa was still alive, my father had already begun sending his parents abroad. It mattered to him that they fly. Neither of them had ever been on a plane. Most people their age on Moti Fali never would be. He wanted his mother and father, a housewife and a farmer, to sit on a plane and see the world. I went with both my grandparents to the UAE. My father took the two of them to Thailand. There were other trips after that, to other countries, more than I kept track of.

I have wondered since how much of it was for them and how much was for the street. A farmer's son from Pal Gam, flying his unschooled parents to foreign countries. In that community, in that decade, it was still a big achievement. It was the kind of thing the Samaj noticed and passed along. Shambu took his parents abroad. Whatever else those trips were, they were also that.

I think about one more thing. The father he put on a plane was the same father he tied to a bed. The Samaj heard about the plane. It never heard about the bed.

The trips I took changed me. I stood in places that ran on rules I had never been taught, and I came home holding a few of them. New ways to think about money, about women, about what a family owes the people inside it. That is what travel is for. A person goes out to bring something back. Otherwise, why buy the ticket?

My father travelled more than I did. More countries. More airports. More ways of living, seen with his own eyes. Did any of it change him? Did he learn anything? Did he really see the new world he was standing in? I have looked hard for what he brought back. I have never found it. His mind left Surat and came back exactly the size it had been. He flew home to the same kitchen, and the same two women working in it, and he expected what he had always expected.

---

Money was the clearest proof of that. Abroad, he spent. At home, he would not. Not a rupee more than he had to, on anything.

There was one exception, and it never changed. He paid our school fees without an argument, on time, in full, every year. His own education had cost him more than he ever explained to us. He was not going to let his children start where he had started. On that, I have never had a bad word for him.

Everything else, we got from somewhere else. Our clothes came from my mother's sister, my Masi. She was well-off, and she had a big heart to match. She was glad to help. So year after year, the shoes and the shirts and the small things a growing child needs came from her hands, not my father's. He never once seemed troubled by this. Why would he be? It was less for him to pay for. He had grown up going without. To him, need was normal. If someone else covered it, that was simply good luck.

My sister and I only named the pattern later, once we were old enough to see its shape. He paid the school fees, and then he stepped out of the frame. Everything after that became our mother's department. The raising of us. The daily cost of two children. The clothes, the doctor, and the thousand small decisions. He had done his part. He would have said so himself.

There is a kind of father this country produces in large numbers. He pays for the education. He takes the credit for it at every wedding. He hands the rest of the child to the mother. My father was that kind. He believed it was enough. He believed it was correct.

---

The money kept growing. His hand stayed closed, and his temper never cooled. The richer the family got, the clearer it became that neither habit had ever been about money.

> त्रिविधं नरकस्येदं द्वारं नाशनमात्मनः ।
> कामः क्रोधस्तथा लोभस्तस्मादेतत्त्रयं त्यजेत् ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 16, Verse 21*
>
> **Translation:** "Three are the gates of this hell, and each one destroys the self: desire, anger, and greed. A person should let go of all three."

In the last chapter, I set another verse from the Gita beside my father. It was the staircase that leads from craving down to anger, and from anger down to ruin. This verse names the company anger keeps. Desire, anger, and greed stand side by side in it. Krishna calls them three gates, and he says a person should turn away from every one.

My father walked toward them his whole life and called it discipline. He only chose forms the Samaj would never fault him for. His greed came out as thrift, and a poor man's son is praised for thrift. His anger, for years, came out on his own father's body. Once Sukhdev papa was gone, most of it had one place left to land.

It would be wrong to say the beating began when Sukhdev papa died. It only lost its old target. My father had been raising his hand to my mother through all the same years he was raising it to his father. Now she was the one who took most of it.

Any argument could end that way. A different opinion. A choice she made without asking him first. A tone he did not like. Every person has a mind of their own. He never allowed my mother hers. The size of the disagreement never decided the size of his answer. As far as I could ever tell, the logic underneath was simple and fixed. He was right, always, about everything. A wife was staff. And a man does not lose an argument to the staff.

The Mahabharata has a different idea of what a wife is.

> नास्ति भार्यासमो बन्धुर्नास्ति भार्यासमा गतिः ।
> नास्ति भार्यासमो लोके सहायो धर्मसाधनः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Chapter 142, Verse 10*
>
> **Translation:** "There is no kin like a wife. There is no refuge like a wife. In this world, there is no helper on the path of dharma like a wife."

The one who says this is not a king or a sage. He is a bird. Bhishma tells Yudhishthira the story of a pigeon who lives in a tree with his wife. One evening a storm comes, and she does not return to the nest. The pigeon sits on his branch in the wind and the rain and grieves for her. Without her, he says, his house is empty. Then he says this verse.

A bird in an old story knew what a wife was. My father had a law degree, and to him a wife was staff.

The pigeon was crying for a wife who had not come home. My mother was home every day of her married life. She was in that kitchen before he woke and after he slept. Every single day, he had the thing that bird was grieving for. He raised his hand to her.

---

I am not going to lay out every time he did it. I could not if I wanted to. There were more of these than there ever were with Sukhdev papa, spread over more years. I have lost most of the reasons he gave. What stayed is the pattern.

He slapped her. He hit her on the body. Sometimes he used a belt. He said things about her parents and the home she came from. I will not put those words on the page. They were the same kind of words he had used on his own father, now aimed at the woman he had married.

What happened to my mother has a name. It is domestic violence. I did not learn that name at home. At home, it had no name at all. It was only something that happened.

It happened in the bedroom. It happened in the main room. Once it happened on a family trip to Goa, in front of relatives. The place was never the point. The anger was the point, and it did not care who was in the room.

Most of the time, the room had my sister and me in it. He never sent us out. Not once. What it might do to a child to watch this, year after year, was never something he weighed. My sister was small. She would cry and say it out loud. "Papa, please. Don't hit Mummy." It went nowhere. It was like speaking to a shut door.

> सम्राज्ञी श्वशुरे भव सम्राज्ञी श्वश्र्वां भव ।
> ननान्दरि सम्राज्ञी भव सम्राज्ञी अधि देवृषु ॥
> — *Rigveda, Mandala 10, Hymn 85, Verse 46 (Surya's Bridal Hymn)*
>
> **Translation:** "Be a queen over your husband's father, a queen over your husband's mother, a queen over his sister, a queen over his brothers."

That verse is not from a philosophy lecture. It is from the wedding itself. It is among the oldest things our tradition says out loud, spoken over the bride while the fire is still lit. It tells her that the house she is walking into is hers to rule. She enters it as a queen, not as a servant.

In our house, it ran the other way. The two brides who came into it were made to serve. My father took pride in his religion. He had stood at that fire himself. Then he brought a wife home and spent nearly thirty years making sure she understood that she was the lowest person under his roof.

I want to be plain about what I think, because my father never was. Being the stronger person in the room does not make a man right. A wife who cannot hit back has not lost the argument. A disagreement between two adults does not have a winner who gets to use his hands. None of this is complicated. I understood it as a child. He was a grown man with a law degree, and he never did.

---

One Sunday holds all of it together.

My mother was up first, the way she was every day. By the middle of the morning the house was swept and the washing was done. Lunch for the whole house was cooked and covered. Then she changed her clothes to go out. Her brother was coming to collect her.

Her father had sold a piece of land. He was an old man, and he wanted to put money into his daughters' hands himself, while he was alive to see it. He was glad to do it. There was nothing shameful in it. There is no rule, anywhere, in any book, that says a woman may not accept a gift from the house she was born in. The money was hers. Not the family's. Not my father's. Hers.

Like so much else in her life, it needed my father's permission. He would not give it.

His reason had nothing to do with what the family needed. It was about what Moti Fali would say if word got out. "People will say I cannot provide for my own house. People will say I live off my wife's father. Only a man with nothing takes money from his in-laws." He said these things to her in the main room that morning, louder each time. Her cooked lunch went cold under its lids. Her brother waited.

I was there. I was old enough to know that what he was doing was completely wrong. I was still too small to speak against my father. So I stood there and said nothing.

Then he hit her. On a Sunday, in front of his children, over money that was never going to touch his hands.

> यत्र धर्मो ह्यधर्मेण सत्यं यत्रानृतेन च ।
> हन्यते प्रेक्षमाणानां हतास्तत्र सभासदः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Chapter 93, Verse 48*
>
> **Translation:** "Where righteousness is struck down by wrongdoing, and truth by a lie, while the assembly sits and watches it happen, that assembly goes down with it."

Krishna says this in the court of the Kauravas, when he comes to plead for peace before the war. He is carrying a message from the Pandavas, and he delivers it to a hall full of elders. They are the same elders who once sat in that court while Draupadi was dragged in and shamed. They had stayed in their seats that day and found reasons not to stand. The verse does not let them off. It says the watchers go down with the crime.

There were relatives watching in Goa. There were two children watching in our main room that Sunday. I was one of them. In that epic hall, one man did get to his feet and object, and the louder men around him talked him down. His name was Vikarna. I was not even Vikarna. I was a boy who watched, and stayed quiet, and carried it out of that room.

She left with her brother. She took the money. She was right to, and it is one of the few times in this story I get to say that plainly about anyone.

---

Here is what became of that money.

Years down the line, the money my father would not be seen accepting became the money he lived on. Quietly, without ever announcing it, he stopped putting his salary into the household. The groceries came out of my mother's account. So did the vegetables, the milk, the gas cylinder, the medicines, the doctor, her own clothes, our pocket money, and the occasional meal from a restaurant.

Her father's land had turned into cash. Now that cash turned into the daily running of my father's house, a little more every month. He kept his own pay. A working man with a steady accounting job had handed the whole upkeep of his family to his wife's inheritance. As far as anyone could see, he felt completely fine about it.

My mother was a housewife. Without a word being said, she had become the provider of the house too. For years she had done a maid's work there without a maid's pay. Now she was paying the bills as well.

> कर्मेन्द्रियाणि संयम्य य आस्ते मनसा स्मरन् ।
> इन्द्रियार्थान्विमूढात्मा मिथ्याचारः स उच्यते ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 3, Verse 6*
>
> **Translation:** "The man who holds his hands still from action while his mind sits turning over the very things he claims to have given up is fooling himself. He is called a hypocrite."

Krishna is describing a man who makes a show of giving something up and goes on wanting it in private. He is not talking about a household budget. But the mechanism is the same. My father built a whole life out of things he was seen to refuse. Meat. Alcohol. Cigarettes. And help of any kind, from anyone, because needing help was for the weak. In public, that last refusal was total. In private, he dropped it the moment it suited him. The person it cost was always my mother.

The Gita has one word for a man whose outside and inside do not match. *Mithyachara.* A hypocrite. I did not reach for that word to wound him. It is simply the accurate one.

My mother never once put it to him as a debt. I do not think the thought ever came to her. She believed she was taking care of her family, and taking care of her family was her whole day. So the money left her hands without a second look. That is how she is built. She gives without counting. She married a man who counts everything and gives nothing back, and she never changed to match him.

---

My sister and I did change. We had no choice.

We stopped waiting for a different father to arrive. We had met the real one. We arranged ourselves around him the way people arrange themselves around the weather. We moved a little further back from him every year. By the time we were teenagers, there was no warmth left in the bond to protect. There was only distance, and a constant quiet reading of his mood.

We did not love him. I want to be exact about that, because it is not a thing a son says lightly. We feared him. The fear crowded out everything a child is supposed to feel for a father. He did that himself, over years. He never tried to be loved by his children. He tried to be obeyed by them. How far he took that, when it was pointed straight at my sister and me, is the rest of this book.

Both of us still carry those years. Even now, on our bad nights, a raised voice at the wrong hour can put either of us right back in that main room. My sister says the same. That is the inheritance he actually left us. Not the land.

---

The calendars changed. Nothing else did.

I moved into higher secondary school, and then into college. He went on hitting my mother whenever he decided it was called for. He acted as though I were still too small to matter in the room. I was not. Somewhere in those years I began to answer him. I raised my voice back. I told him, in plain words, that he was wrong. I told him a husband was not allowed to do this, and that it had to stop. He was not used to being spoken to that way inside his own house. It did not move him an inch.

Once, I put myself between them. I understood very clearly that if he took one more step toward her, I was going to put him on the floor. I meant it completely. He saw that I meant it.

As a boy, on that Sunday, I had watched and stayed quiet. This time I stood up. It was years too late for the Sunday. It was not too late for her.

His grown son was squared up to him. Even then, nothing in his face showed that he understood what he had spent twenty years doing. It had not reached him. Anger came first for him. It came ahead of his wife, and ahead of both his children. It always had.

And through all of it, out on the street, he was still the educated one. The modern one. The man who was so polite to the women on Moti Fali, and so quick to praise their cooking at a function. The steady, decent, self-made man the Samaj sent for when it needed someone reliable at the front of a hall. That tie only pulled tighter as the years went on. The more of himself he spent at home on rage, the more he seemed to need the Samaj's good opinion. The two grew together.

---

Through all of it, my mother forgave him. Every time.

She never stopped calling him her husband. She always took him back. She never once sat down and added up what he owed her. The money. The years. The plain safety a person is supposed to have inside her own bedroom. The account stayed open, and one-sided, for the whole of her married life. She was at peace with that in a way I still am not.

> एकः क्षमावतां दोषो द्वितीयो नोपलभ्यते ।
> यदेनं क्षमया युक्तमशक्तं मन्यते जनः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Vidura Niti), Chapter 33, Verse 47*
>
> **Translation:** "Those who forgive have one fault, and no second can be found. People take the one who forgives to be weak."

This is Vidura again, from the same long night with the sleepless king that I quoted in the last chapter. He does not call forgiveness a weakness. He says people mistake it for one. Later that night he says it straight. Violence is the strength of the wicked. Forgiveness is the strength of the good.

My father made the exact mistake Vidura describes. He looked at a woman who forgave him every time, and he saw someone weak. He saw someone who would always take it. He had the two kinds of strength the wrong way round. His was the kind Vidura gives to the wicked. Hers was the other kind.

It took him more than twenty years to find out how strong she was. That day comes later in this book.

Until then, the Samaj went on using its word for my father. Ideal. We had watched a different man, in rooms the Samaj was never allowed into.

A street measures a man by how he speaks to other men's wives. A house measures him by the woman in his own kitchen. The street gave my father full marks. It never saw the kitchen.

Once, her family offered help that might have embarrassed him in front of that street, and he refused it, loudly. Everything the street could not see, he took. Her work. Her money. Her safety. In the end he took all of it. He just made sure no one saw his hands.
