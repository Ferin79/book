# Chapter Six: The Earning Cow

What was waiting for her was marriage.

I knew it was coming, because it had already come for me. In the fourth chapter, I wrote about the year my father chose my wife. The five biodatas. The daily calls. The one filter. I wrote that I lost.

Now it was my sister's turn.

The first five chapters of this book are named after people. So is this one. But I did not choose the name. My father did. It is what he called his daughter in the middle of this fight. I will come to it.

---

It began while she was still a student.

MBBS is a heavy course. It is years of lectures, hospital wards, and exams. Even after the studying is done, a student has to serve one more year of practice before the degree is complete. Nobody gets it easily.

I called Janvi often in those years, from Halifax. Every time, she was studying. I never once found her taking time for herself. It was lectures, or books, or the hostel. That was her whole life.

The hostel was a weight of its own. In the last chapter, I wrote that she left home glad to go, and that was true. But she had never lived away from home before. She missed her mother. She had to do everything by herself for the first time, on top of the hardest studies of her life. It was brutal for her.

My father saw none of it. He was not looking at his daughter. He was looking at a calendar.

In our Samaj, most girls are married by 24 or 25. So in his mind, 23 was the right age for an engagement, with the wedding to follow once her studies were done. Janvi was about to turn 23. That was all the reasoning there was. Twenty-three is not old. I don't think so, and most people I know don't think so. My father believed the Samaj's clock was the only clock.

Nobody asked Janvi whether she was ready. Nobody had asked me, either. When he decides it is time, it is time.

---

Janvi told him it was not time.

She told him many times, and she gave him reasons. She was in the middle of her degree. Her days were already full from morning to night. An engagement brings a new family with it, and she had no time to give that family, or even the boy. She wanted to finish MBBS first. She had come too far to put her career at risk now.

They were good reasons. Every one of them fell on deaf ears.

His pressure on her was even heavier than it had been on me. I think I know why. A son stays in the house. A daughter is sent to another one, and in his mind that has to be done on schedule. So he would not wait.

The calls began. They came every day. I knew those calls. In the fourth chapter, I wrote that the cage had followed me to Canada, and that it rang. Now it rang in a hostel room in Dahod. He shouted at her. He used foul language on her. She was a medical student, alone with her books, a long way from home, and that was what came down the phone from her father.

In that same chapter, I quoted Vidura. A wound from an arrow heals, he says. A wound from cruel words never does. Janvi was given one of those wounds every day. Then she was expected to open her books and study.

---

It could not go on like that without doing damage, and it did not.

Her studies began to suffer. Her mind began to suffer. So she did something that was very hard for her. In the last chapter, I wrote that she grew up never asking for anything. This time, she asked.

"Papa, stop. This is affecting my studies. It is affecting my mental health."

His answer was one sentence. "*Mari samme aa badhu nai lavanu.*"

Don't bring all this in front of me. I don't care.

I want to stay with that sentence. I think it is the most honest thing my father has ever said to either of his children.

This was not a stranger complaining to him. This was the daughter I wrote about in the last chapter. In her drop year, under his pressure, she had said more than once that she wanted to end her life. Our mother had kept her safe. Now the same daughter was telling him, in plain words, that he was making her unwell. He told her not to bring it to him.

> વૈષ્ણવજન તો તેને રે કહીયે, જે પીડ પરાઈ જાણે રે ।
> પરદુઃખે ઉપકાર કરે તોયે, મન અભિમાન ન આણે રે ॥
> — *Narsinh Mehta, "Vaishnav Jan To"*
>
> **Translation:** "Call that one a person of God who knows the pain of another. He helps those who are in sorrow, and still lets no pride enter his mind."

Every Gujarati knows this song. Narsinh Mehta wrote it more than five hundred years ago, in our own language. It is still sung in homes, in schools, and in temples across Gujarat. It is not a song for one sect. It is the simplest description we have of a godly person. And it does not begin with fasting, or with prayer. It begins with one test. Does he know another person's pain?

My father prayed every morning and every evening. His own daughter carried her pain to him, in so many words, and he told her to take it away.

The last verse of the same song says what else such a person is. He is free of greed. He has put away anger. Much of this book has been about my father's greed and my father's anger. By the best-loved song in his own language, he fails the first line and the last.

Something ended for Janvi that day. A father who hears that he is harming his child's health, and does not care, has stopped being worth anything to that child. He lost his place in her eyes. Like me, she still calls him Papa, because she has to. The word holds nothing now.

---

After that, she stopped coming home.

For a girl, her parents' home is supposed to be the safest place in the world. It is supposed to stay that way even after she is married. For Janvi, it was the one place she wanted to avoid.

Festivals came. Long weekends came. National holidays came. The hostel emptied as the other students went home to their families. Janvi stayed. She chose an empty hostel over her own house.

It was not that she did not love her family. She loved her mother. She hated her father. She would rather cry alone in her room than go home to him.

In the last chapter, I wrote that the Lakshmi of our house had left it. Now she would not even visit.

I watched all of this from the other side of the world. It is a painful thing to see happen to a sister from that far away, and to be able to do nothing.

---

She could not stay away every time. Sometimes she was made to come. There were boys for her to see.

On those days, my father decided everything. He decided what looked good on her. He decided how her hair should be done. He decided what colour of dress she would wear to meet the boy. In the fourth chapter, I wrote that he chose my clothes for every event. With Janvi, he went further. She was being arranged, like something to be shown.

That is how he sees women. In the third chapter, I wrote that in his mind women were made for work, and made to be under him. To him they are objects, and an object can be arranged. He is not the only man in India who thinks this way. My uncle Praful thinks the same. Between them, they believed a woman's life was theirs to control however they liked.

---

That control did not begin with the viewings. It began earlier, with her phone.

Janvi was young, and she was in college. By then, everyone her age in India was on social media. It was the trend. Her friends posted photos on Instagram. They put up stories on WhatsApp. It was an ordinary part of being young. My father never understood any of it.

He did not allow her to post photos on Instagram. He did not allow her to put up stories on WhatsApp. She was not even allowed a DP, the small profile photo that sits beside a person's name on WhatsApp. "Why use social media?" he would say. "It is a waste of time. Focus on your studies."

From time to time, he also checked her phone. He went through it to keep an eye on her, and on her contacts. He was not checking because he worried for her. He was checking for boys. He wanted to be sure she was not talking to any boy in college, and that there was no affair.

In the fourth chapter, I wrote that I had no phone of my own until I finished twelfth grade, and I wrote why. He could not trust his own son. He trusted his daughter no more than that. She had a phone, and he searched it.

If he had truly believed that social media was a waste of time, that would at least have been a belief. He did not believe it. I found that out when the talk of my own engagement began.

Suddenly he had no problem with social media at all. He shared the girls' profiles with me gladly. He found them fascinating. "Look at her photo. She looks perfect for you." "Wow, she travels a lot." "She is so fashionable." He said these things to me himself.

Every one of those girls was somebody's daughter. She posted her photos. She posted her travels. She showed her style to anyone who looked. In another man's daughter, he admired all of it. In his own, he had forbidden it, down to one small photo beside her name. If it was so good in someone else's daughter, why was it banned for his?

He was not against social media for himself, either. In the last chapter, I wrote about his Facebook. The Samaj had watched his passport fill with stamps there. He posted there when Janvi cracked NEET. It was never a waste of time when it showed him well. His daughter could not put her own face on her own phone. He put her result on his page.

Hypocrisy, once more. And the reason behind it is the one this book keeps arriving at. He cared about his image. He did not want one photo, one story, or one message that might spoil it. He never trusted anyone. Not even his own children.

So he kept her face off her own phone, and on the day of a viewing he chose her dress and her hair. Who saw his daughter, and when, was his to decide.

Janvi is a grown woman now, and she will soon be a doctor. He still controls everything for her, to this day. It still surprises me.

When her own turn came, the photographs were of boys. Before each meeting came a boy's photograph, or his biodata. And that is where the arguments began.

---

Sometimes Janvi's answer was simple. "I don't like his looks."

That is a fair thing to say. If boys are being brought to her, she at least has the right to say which ones she likes.

But if the boy's family was rich, and my father liked the family, he had an answer ready. "What is in the looks? After you have a child, what are you going to do with his looks?"

I have thought about that sentence for a long time. It is worth taking apart, because a whole idea of marriage is packed inside it.

It says, first, that a marriage exists to produce a child. Once the child arrives, the marriage has done its job. Whether the wife can bear to look at her husband does not matter after that. Liking him was only a stage to get through.

It says that a woman's eyes have an expiry date. She may look at her husband until the baby comes. After that she is to look at the baby, the kitchen, and the house. Her own liking is no longer needed.

And it forgets how long a marriage is. A child fills twenty years of it, perhaps. Then the child grows up and goes. My father should know that better than anyone. Both of his children left his house. A marriage goes on for decades after that. Two people sit in a home and look at each other. That is the part he was telling her not to think about.

I do not think he was trying to be clever. I think he was describing the only marriage he knows. I wrote about that marriage in the third chapter.

There is one more thing. He did not really believe that looks do not matter. When the photographs were of girls for his son, looks were what he pointed to. "Look at her photo. She looks perfect for you." And on the day of a viewing, he chose the colour of Janvi's dress and the way her hair was done. So looks mattered a great deal. They mattered when a woman was the one being looked at. They only stopped mattering when she was the one looking.

---

Sometimes her objection was a different one. "I don't like his background," she would say. She meant his studies, and his career.

He had an answer for that too. "You will not get everything. You have to compromise on something."

Most parents wear themselves out so that a daughter will not have to compromise. They search. They ask around. They wait. They do their best at the time of her marriage, so that she can live in peace in her new home for the rest of her life. My father was doing the opposite. He was bringing a boy to his daughter and telling her to settle.

So I ask the plain question. Why should she compromise on anything?

Compromise is for a person who is short of something. What was Janvi short of? She is studying medicine. She won that seat by her own effort, against everything I described in the last chapter. She is beautiful. Her family is financially secure. By the Samaj's own way of counting, she has everything a family looks for. If a girl like that has to settle, then who does not?

There is also the matter of what he was asking her to give up. The boy's looks. His education. His career. Later, his age. Every item on her list was open to compromise. The one item on his list never was. The family had to be rich. He never offered to bend on that.

So it was not a compromise at all. In a compromise, both sides give something up. He was asking her to give up everything she wanted, so that he would not have to give up the one thing he wanted.

Then there is everything he had spent years building. In the last chapter, I wrote about his one line for her. "*Tare doctor j banvanu chhe.*" You will become a doctor, and nothing else. He drove her through NEET twice for it. He wanted the best profile in the Samaj for his daughter. Now she had it, and the first thing he told her was to settle for less.

Then what was the profile for? It was never meant to give her more choices. It was meant to give him a better story.

---

When his own arguments failed, he borrowed someone else's life.

"Look at Khushi," he would say. Khushi is a girl from his mother's side of the family. "She is a doctor too, just like you. She got engaged where her father told her to. She accepted her father's wish. You should do the same."

I have nothing against Khushi's decision. She may be happy with it, and I hope she is. But one girl's choice is not a law for every girl. It is not a universal truth that a daughter must follow her father blindly.

And the two cases were not the same. Her family's situation is different from ours. They live in a village, and they have less than we do. In the Samaj's marriage market, that means fewer proposals to choose from. That is the Samaj's arithmetic, not mine. Her family weighed their own situation and made the decision that suited it. Janvi's situation was different, so the example proved nothing.

He would not hear any of this. "Whatever I am doing is right," he kept saying. "Just do what I say."

He was not alone, either. His brother joined his side. My uncle Praful could have given him good advice. He could have told him to look at the boy, and not at the boy's family. He did the reverse. He talked about status, and he poured oil on the fire. In this part of the story, he is the silent villain.

But my fight is not with my uncle. My fight is with the one man whose first duty was to protect his children, and who used them for his name in the Samaj instead.

---

That man had one more argument, and he was proud of it.

"In our time, we never even saw the girl. Our parents decided who we married. At least I am giving you a choice."

He said it as if he were doing her a favour.

Janvi answered him.

"What choice? You are pushing me toward one or two boys that you like. Not me. You like their family's status. What do you actually know about him? What is his nature? What kind of person is he? You are falling for the balance sheet of his family. You are not looking at the boy at all. Is he good for me? I am not marrying his family. I am marrying him. What do you know about him?"

He had no answer. He had never looked into the boys themselves. He had looked into their families' money and their standing. He had one test. The family had to be wealthier than ours.

When I hear her words, I hear my own. In the fourth chapter, my father gave me a ten-minute speech about Vrunda's family, and I asked him one question. Am I marrying her family, or the girl? A couple of years later, my sister asked him the same thing. He has two children. Both of them had to ask their father that question. Neither of them got an answer.

The words she chose have stayed with me. A balance sheet. My father has kept accounts all his working life. His own daughter looked at him and saw that he was doing the same job at home. He was reading a family the way he reads a ledger. Assets, and nothing else on the page.

The choice he offered was narrow in another way too. Janvi did not want to marry a doctor. That is her own wish, and I respect it. He did not. The one or two boys he brought were doctors. In his mind, no other field is worth as much. His daughter is a doctor, so she must go to a doctor's family. To him it was obvious.

She did not like any of them. Some were far older than her. Some she simply did not find good-looking. Each time she said no, he grew more furious.

"Why are you saying no to these families? They are good for you. They are wealthy. There are no more doctor boys left in our Samaj. What will you do now? You have no options left."

> का त्वा मुकुन्द महती कुलशीलरूपविद्यावयोद्रविणधामभिरात्मतुल्यम् ।
> धीरा पतिं कुलवती न वृणीत कन्या काले नृसिंह नरलोकमनोऽभिरामम् ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavata Purana, Book 10, Chapter 52, Verse 38*
>
> **Translation:** "O Mukunda, in family, character, looks, learning, age, wealth, and standing, you are equalled only by yourself. What girl of good family, noble and clear-headed, would not choose you as her husband when the time came? You are a lion among men, and the delight of every heart."

These lines are from a letter. The girl who wrote it is Rukmini, the princess of Vidarbha. The Bhagavata Purana calls her a part of Lakshmi herself, born on earth. She had heard about Krishna, and she had decided he was the right husband for her. Her family was willing. Her brother Rukmi was not. He overruled them all and fixed her marriage to another king. So she wrote to Krishna herself, and sent the letter to Dwarka with a Brahmin she trusted.

She counts seven things in this verse. Family. Character. Looks. Learning. Age. Wealth. Standing. She weighs all seven. Looks are on her list. So is learning. So is age. The verse before this one speaks of his looks as well, and she says she feels no shame in saying so.

Those were Janvi's three objections. His looks. His studies. His age. My father treated each one as a girl being difficult. In our own scripture, Lakshmi puts all three in a letter to God.

I should be fair to the verse. Rukmini is praising Krishna, who has all seven. No ordinary boy does. But the verse shows who does the weighing, and what she is allowed to weigh. My father's list had two of the seven on it. Wealth and standing.

One more word in that verse matters. *Vrinita.* The girl chooses. Our house welcomed Janvi as Lakshmi on the day she was born. When Lakshmi's own family tried to choose for her, she did not accept it.

---

"You have no options left." About that, at least, he was right. It is worth asking why.

Our Samaj has a bigger problem than my father. It is the gap between its money and its education.

In the third chapter, I wrote about the day the city reached Pal Gam, and what that did to the price of land. It did not happen only to us. It happened to almost every family around us. Land that had been farmed for generations was sold for the city's growth. Families became rich almost overnight.

The money came. The education did not. Most families in our Samaj never gave schooling much weight. Many of the children I grew up around left college without finishing. Many sit at home without work. They live on the interest from money their families lend out. Nobody has to work, so few do.

Why is that my family's problem? Because my sister and I are educated, and educated families in our Samaj are few. We both had to choose from very little. When my turn came, I wanted a girl who had studied science. Almost eight out of ten had not. For Janvi, the whole Samaj held only a couple of doctor families, and she said no to most of the few boys in them.

So here is my question. Our Samaj chose not to educate itself. Must my sister pay for that with her life? Must she accept whoever is left? If the answer is yes, then what was the point of MBBS? She could have dropped out like everyone else and taken it easy.

> किं कुलेन विशालेन विद्याहीनेन देहिनाम् ।
> दुष्कुलं चापि विदुषो देवैरपि स पूज्यते ॥
> — *Chanakya Niti, Chapter 8, Verse 19*
>
> **Translation:** "What use is a great family to people who have no learning? A learned man is honoured even by the gods, even if his family is a low one."

In the fourth chapter, I quoted Chanakya to thank my father. Chanakya calls a parent an enemy if he does not educate his children. By that measure, I wrote, my father was never my enemy. He sent us to an English medium school. He paid for the tuitions. He was the one who said his daughter would be a doctor and nothing else. I gave him that credit then, and I give it again now.

But this is the same Chanakya. A great family without learning, he says, is worth nothing. A learned man from a small family is honoured even by the gods.

My father raised his two children by the first verse. Then he tried to marry them against the second. He made us different from the families around us. Then he demanded that we choose as if we were the same.

---

There was one boy who was different. My father did not bring him. Janvi did.

This part of the story turns on a single word. In Gujarati, it is *jameen*. Land. I have come to believe that this word is dearer to my father than his own children. Here is why.

Janvi has a friend named Jash. He is from our own Samaj. She has known him for four or five years. Our father had brought her no real options. In truth, he had brought her none at all. So she decided to consider Jash for marriage, and she told our father.

He was furious, as always. He spoke to her rudely. He used foul language. His anger was not about the boy. It was that she had brought a name to him without his approval.

"How dare you," he said. "I do not approve of him. Are you having an affair with him?"

In the fourth chapter, I wrote about the word *lafda*. An affair. The kind of trouble people whisper about. My father lectured me against it for years, for the sake of his image. He had searched Janvi's phone for it. Now he threw the same idea at her, to her face. She had come to him openly, with a boy's name, the way a daughter is supposed to. He answered by questioning her character.

A family protects its daughter's name. When people talk, her father is the one who stands in front of her. In our house, the first person to put a stain on her name was her father.

Janvi kept her head. "What is the problem with him?" she asked. Then she told him who Jash is. He is a computer engineer. He graduated from the same university in Canada as her brother. He is well-behaved and mature. He is her own age. She has known him for years. His family is decent, and well known in the Samaj.

My father went away with that. Some days later, he came back with his answer.

"I do not approve of this boy. You cannot marry him. He is a perfect boy. But his family does not have enough land."

He admitted all of it. A good family. A respected one. An excellent background. A perfect boy. Only one thing was missing.

Then he explained, at length, why land matters. How will he support you in the future, with no land money behind him? Nobody becomes rich doing a nine-to-five job. What if the two of you want to travel abroad? Can your salaries pay for that? You need land money. You need a big inheritance to live well in India.

Janvi answered him. "Why do you care about his land? That will be ours to worry about. We will deal with it in our own lives. We will not come to you for help. He may have less land, but he will keep me happy. I will be happy with him. He is right for me."

Once again, her words fell on deaf ears. His mind was made up.

> अयं हि सर्वधर्माणां धर्मश्चिन्त्यतमो मतः ।
> कीदृशाय प्रदेया स्यात्कन्येति वसुधाधिप ॥
> शीलवृत्ते समाज्ञाय विद्यां योनिं च कर्म च ।
> अद्भिरेव प्रदातव्या कन्या गुणवते वरे ।
> ब्राह्मणानां सतामेष धर्मो नित्यं युधिष्ठिर ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Chapter 44, Verses 2–3*
>
> **Translation:** "Of all duties, this is held to be the one that needs the most thought, O king: to what kind of man should a daughter be given? Her father should find out the man's character and conduct, his learning, his family, and his work. Then the daughter should be given, with water alone, to a groom of good qualities. This has always been the way of good Brahmins, Yudhishthira."

I have quoted this teaching before. In the third chapter, Bhishma lay on his bed of arrows and told Yudhishthira that a family whose women grieve is already lost. This comes from the same teaching, two chapters earlier. Yudhishthira asks him what lies at the root of all the duties of a household. Bhishma's answer begins here. Of everything a householder must do, he says, one duty needs the most thought. To what kind of man should a daughter be given?

Then he gives the list. Character and conduct. Learning. Family. Work. A father must find these out, and give his daughter to a man of good qualities. Bhishma calls this the way of the best Brahmins. It is the first and highest of the kinds of marriage he goes on to describe.

Janvi's answer sits line for line beside that list. Well-behaved and mature. That is character and conduct. A computer engineer, with a degree from a university abroad. That is learning. A decent family, well known in the Samaj. That is family. A profession of his own. That is work. She went down Bhishma's list without knowing it, and Jash passed every line. My father agreed that he did. "He is a perfect boy."

Bhishma's list has no land on it.

The verse also says the daughter should be given "with water alone." It means the old rite, where a father pours water as he gives his daughter away. Nothing is bought, and nothing is sold. A few lines later, Bhishma describes the opposite kind of marriage. In it, wealth decides, and the girl's family is tempted by it. He calls that the way of the demons, and he says it must never be done.

Nobody was offering my father money for his daughter. But "tempted by wealth" is the most exact description I have found of how the other boys reached our house.

---

So why land? Why does that one word outweigh a perfect boy?

I have thought about it, and I believe the answer lies in our own family's story. We did not rise because of my father's degree. We did not rise because of his salary. We rose because of land. In the third chapter, I wrote that money came in that nobody had worked a harvest for. The same thing happened all around us. So in our Samaj, land became the measure of a family. A family with land never has to work. A family without it works for its money, and in the Samaj's eyes that makes it lower.

That is the rule my father was applying. And he, of all people, should have been the last man to apply it.

In the first chapter, I wrote that the land his father farmed fed the family and never made anyone rich. I wrote that other men his age had family money to lean on, and he had no such cushion. He took a salaried job, and he built everything he had from it. The street admired him for exactly that.

"Nobody becomes rich doing a nine-to-five job." My father has done a nine-to-five job all his life. He was describing himself. As a young man, he had a degree, a salary, and a family whose land was worth very little. By the test he set for Jash, he would have failed. The boy he rejected was the boy he had once been.

He also knows very well what land money can do for a man. In the third chapter, I wrote about the money my mother received when her father sold his land. My father would not be seen accepting it. Then he ran his whole house on it for years, and kept his own salary. So when he says a man needs land money behind him, he is speaking from experience. It was just never his own land.

And he asked whether two salaries could pay for a life with travel in it. I work a nine-to-five job in software. It pays for my life in Canada, the country he once dreamed of reaching. Jash studied at the same university I did.

Vidura had an answer for this fear long ago. On that same sleepless night, he tells the king that men with thousands live, and men with hundreds live too. Let go of the wanting, he says. Nobody fails to live. Janvi said the same thing in her own words. It will be ours to worry about. We will not come to you.

So it was never really about how his daughter would live. I understood that only when he rejected Jash. This was not only control. It was his image again. He will not send his daughter into a family that stands lower than ours in the Samaj's eyes. In the fourth chapter, he could already hear people say, "Look where Rakesh got his son engaged." He wanted to hear it a second time.

---

There are fathers who would rather hear something else.

My father gave Janvi the example of Khushi. Let me give an example of my own.

There is a family in our own society whose daughter was engaged twice. Both times, it did not work out. The third time, she was engaged into a lower-middle-class family. People talked, as people do. Her father stood up in front of all of them.

"I will take care of my daughter's future," he said. "I am here for that. The rest of you do not need to talk about it, or worry about it."

That is what I call a real father. He stood behind his daughter's decision. He was ready to do anything, only to see her happy. My father is ready to do anything too. But he does it for his own ego, and for his own name in the Samaj.

In the fourth chapter, I wrote about Savitri's father, King Ashvapati. His daughter chose a man whose family had lost its kingdom and lived in a forest. They had nothing left. He accepted her choice. That father is in the Mahabharata. This one lives in our own Samaj. So nobody can tell me that such fathers exist only in old books.

---

My father is not such a father. I can prove it with one name he gave his daughter. It is the name at the top of this chapter, and it is the hardest thing in this book for me to write.

Janvi is going to be a doctor. In time she will earn more than Jash, who is an engineer. My father and my uncle Praful looked at that fact, and they found a name for her. An earning cow. A cash cow.

"You are like an earning cow," my father told her. "You will go into Jash's middle-class family, and they will love you, because you bring cash into their house. And we will have lost an earning cow to people lower than us."

There are three insults in that sentence. I want to count all of them.

The first is to Janvi. She gave her youth to that degree. She sat NEET twice. She survived the drop year. She won her seat in spite of him. And when her own father looked at all of it, he saw an animal that gives money.

The second is to Jash's family. Without knowing them, he decided they could only want her for her salary. He could not imagine a family welcoming a girl for herself. A man imagines in others what he would do himself.

The third insult is the one he did not notice. It is to himself. "We will have lost." A person can only lose what he believes he owns. In that one word, he told her what she had been to him all along. She was not a daughter he was giving in marriage. She was an asset he was being asked to part with, to a family that could not match her worth.

His own arguments do not even agree with each other. In the same weeks, he told her two things. He told her that a salaried couple cannot live well, and that she would need a husband's land money to support her. He also told her that her income was so valuable that another family would treasure her for it. Both cannot be true. If she is an earning cow, she does not need anyone's land. If she needs land to live, she is not an earning cow. He said whichever one suited the argument he was losing that day.

In the last chapter, I quoted a hymn to Lakshmi. The singer asks the goddess to come, and he lists what he hopes to gain through her. Gold. Cattle. Horses. Men. I wrote that my father came to treat his daughter the way that hymn treats the goddess, as something that brought him things. The hymn only asks Lakshmi for cattle. My father went one step further. He looked at the Lakshmi of his own house, and he called her the cow.

> अन्योऽप्यथ न विक्रेयो मनुष्यः किं पुनः प्रजाः ।
> अधर्ममूलैर्हि धनैर्न तैरर्थोऽस्ति कश्चन ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Chapter 45, Verse 24*
>
> **Translation:** "Even a stranger must not be sold, for he is a human being. How much less, then, one's own children? Wealth that grows from a wrong root is of no use to anyone."

This is Bhishma again, in the same teaching about daughters and marriage. He is speaking of fathers who take a price for a daughter, and he is harsh about it. He recites old verses that send such a father to hell. Then he turns to an old custom, in which the groom gave the bride's father a pair of cows. Some people said it was only a token. Bhishma does not accept that. Small or large, he says, it is a sale.

A pair of cows. That was the price the old texts argued about, and Bhishma would not allow even that. My father did not take a cow for his daughter. He called his daughter the cow.

I want to be exact here, as I have tried to be all through this book. Nobody was paying my father for Janvi. No money was going to change hands. But he was doing the sum. What the family gains. What the family loses. Who gets the earnings. That sum is the thing Bhishma forbids. A child is not to be counted that way.

In the same chapter, Bhishma says what a daughter is instead. A son is a man's own self, he says, and a daughter is equal to a son.

By Bhishma, my sister is my father's own self. By my father, she is an earning cow.

This is 2026. Those words came from a man who has travelled to ten countries, who holds a law degree, and whose name is respected across our Samaj. My sister did not deserve to hear them.

---

After that, he stopped giving reasons at all.

"Do whatever you can," he told her. "I will not allow anyone you bring to me. I will marry you only where my heart wants."

Janvi had no words left. Each time, she had thought a person could not go any lower than this. Each time, he went lower.

> आत्माभिप्रेतमुत्सृज्य कन्याभिप्रेत एव यः ।
> अभिप्रेता च या यस्य तस्मै देया युधिष्ठिर ।
> गान्धर्वमिति तं धर्मं प्राहुर्धर्मविदो जनाः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Chapter 44, Verse 5*
>
> **Translation:** "Setting aside the man he himself prefers, a father should give his daughter to the man she prefers, and who prefers her, Yudhishthira. Those who know dharma call this the Gandharva way."

Bhishma describes the kinds of marriage one after another. This is the third. The father puts his own wish aside. He gives his daughter to the man she herself wishes for. Bhishma does not call that rebellion. A few lines later, he counts it among the righteous kinds of marriage.

The verse opens with a father setting aside his own wish. My father's sentence closes on his. Only where my heart wants. It is the same sentence, turned inside out.

In the fourth chapter, I quoted the end of the Gita. Krishna teaches Arjuna for seven hundred verses, and then he says, do as you wish. God ends on the other person's wish. My father ends on his own.

---

That is where the story stands. It has no ending yet.

Janvi is still fighting him. So is my mother. So am I.

And this is where the idea for this book began. I was watching, from far away, my father do to my sister what he had already done to the rest of us. For the first time, I saw the pattern whole.

He ruins a life, and then he gets a clean start. Every time. He beat his own father for years, and he went on being Shambu, the son every son should be. He beat his wife and lived on her money, and he went on being the ideal man. He chose my marriage for me, and he went on being the proud father. He broke my sister down in her drop year, and then he posted her success on Facebook.

Four people. My grandfather. My mother. Me. My sister. And that is not the full list. He has brought pain into other lives too.

Each time, the account was wiped clean. The hard part to admit is that we were the ones who wiped it. We stayed silent. We let things go. We forgot, because it was easier to forget. We told ourselves that he is our father.

In the third chapter, I quoted Vidura. People take the one who forgives to be weak. I wrote it about my mother. It was true of all of us. My father took our silence for weakness. He took it as permission.

Enough is enough. I decided the silence would end with me.

In that same chapter, I wrote about the hall where Draupadi was shamed, and the elders who watched from their seats. I wrote that as a boy I was not even Vikarna, the one man who stood up. Years later I stood up once, for my mother. This book is the second time. This time, I am standing where the whole hall can hear.

I want to be clear about what the book is for. It is not written to turn anyone against my father. It is not written to pull a reader to my side. It is written to show the whole of a man, where the world has only been shown half.

> वेदमनूच्याचार्योऽन्तेवासिनमनुशास्ति ।
> सत्यं वद । धर्मं चर । स्वाध्यायान्मा प्रमदः ।
> — *Taittiriya Upanishad, Shiksha Valli, Anuvaka 11, Verse 1*
>
> **Translation:** "When the teaching of the Veda is complete, the teacher instructs his student. Speak the truth. Follow dharma. Do not neglect your study."

This book takes its name from the Taittiriya Upanishad. I quoted the verse at the end of the fourth chapter. Treat your father as a god. Those words belong to what a teacher tells his student on the day the student leaves him. But they are not where the teacher begins. He begins here, a few lines earlier. The very first thing he says is two words long. *Satyam vada.* Speak the truth.

The truth comes first. The father comes after. That is the order in the Upanishad, and it is the order I have tried to keep.

In the first chapter, I wrote that my father kept accounts for a living, and that every entry has two sides. I wrote down the side the street knows. Honest. Hardworking. Devoted. Trustworthy. I said then that none of it was false, and I say it again now. He built himself from nothing. He educated his children. He prays every morning and every evening.

The chapters since then are the other side. An old man's feet. A wife's work, her money, and her safety. A son's choice. A daughter's youth. The Samaj has only ever read the first column. I grew up inside the second.

A balance sheet is honest only when both sides are on the page. That is all this book does. It sets the second column beside the first. Whoever reads it is free to do the sum, and free to reach a different total than mine.

The story is not over. My sister is nobody's earning cow. She is still fighting for the right to choose her own life. My mother is still standing beside her. I am still on the phone, from the other side of the world.

But the silence is over. That part, at least, is finished.
