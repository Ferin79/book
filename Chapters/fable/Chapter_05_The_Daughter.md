# Chapter Five: The Daughter

I ended the last chapter with a choice. I could choose what to keep from my father, and what to leave with him.

My sister never got that much. She had to fight just to leave his house.

My life with him was hard. Hers was harder. In the last chapter, I said that her rules were even stricter than mine, and that her story would get a whole chapter. This is that chapter. It belongs to Janvi. Of everything in this book, it is the part that matters most to me.

---

Janvi was born on November 11, 2002, about a week after Diwali. I was not yet three, so I don't remember the day. But I know what was said, because it is said in every Gujarati home when a baby girl arrives. *Lakshmi aavi.* Lakshmi has come.

In our culture, a daughter is welcomed as Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth. People call her the Lakshmi of the house. They believe good fortune comes in with her.

In our house, it did. While Janvi was still small, the city reached Pal Gam. I wrote about it in the third chapter. The land Sukhdev papa had farmed all his life was suddenly worth more than anyone had imagined. Maybe it was only timing. But the money came after she did.

> तां म आवह जातवेदो लक्ष्मीमनपगामिनीम् ।
> यस्यां हिरण्यं विन्देयं गामश्वं पुरुषानहम् ॥
> — *Sri Sukta (Rigveda Khila), Verse 2*
>
> **Translation:** "O Agni, bring me that Lakshmi who will never leave. Through her, may I gain gold, cattle, horses, and men."

The Sri Sukta is an old hymn to Lakshmi, from a set of extra hymns attached to the Rigveda. It is still recited at Lakshmi puja across India, on the night of Diwali. The singer is honest about what he wants. He asks Agni, the god of fire, for a Lakshmi who will stay. And he asks for what she brings with her. Gold. Cattle. Horses. Men.

Over the years, my father came to treat his daughter the way this hymn treats the goddess. She was something that brought him things. Top marks. Praise in the Samaj. A daughter to show off. The hymn asks for a Lakshmi who will never leave. Ours left. Nineteen years after she was born, Janvi packed her bags and left his house the first chance she got.

This chapter is about the years in between.

---

For most of those years, Janvi and I shared everything. We went to the same English medium school. We went to the same tuitions every afternoon, in different grades. We shared a house, a routine, and a long list of rules. On Moti Fali, I was Shambu's son and she was Shambu's daughter. We shared the fear of our father too.

In many families, a daughter and her father have a special bond. She is the one who can soften him. She is the one he cannot say no to. It was never like that in our house. There was no bond between Janvi and our father. There was only fear.

She had seen everything I had seen. Sukhdev papa crying after a beating, and the glass of water we carried to him together. Our mother being hit, again and again, while Janvi begged him to stop. She learned very early what kind of man our father was. She learned to stay out of his way.

At school, she was brilliant. She was always at the top of her class. Whatever she did, she did it best.

Raising her was easy compared to raising me. I was the naughty one. She was calm and quiet. She never asked for expensive toys. She never asked for new clothes. She hardly asked for anything at all. She lived our parents' dreams, and she was happy with whatever they did for her. She never spoke against their opinion. She never once put her own choice first.

That made her easy to manage, especially for my father. He never had to do anything for her. She was too afraid of him to ask. Her silence looked like contentment. It was fear.

---

That fear only grew when she reached high school. That is when things turned serious.

The lectures started for her too. In the last chapter, I wrote about my father's monthly sessions. Don't smoke. Don't drink. Look at your grandfather. Janvi got her own sessions. The topic was different. The idea was the same.

He knew a girl in our family was not going to smoke or drink. So her lectures went another way. No stupid things at school. No relationships. Only studies. The best marks in the class, every time.

And above everything else, one line. "*Tare doctor j banvanu chhe.*" You will become a doctor, and nothing else.

His reason was the same reason he had with me. Image. A daughter at the top of her class was a daughter he could show off. A daughter on her way to becoming a doctor was something to talk about in the Samaj. With a better profile, she would make a better story for him to tell.

It did not stop there. It only got worse.

---

It got worse in eleventh and twelfth grade. We both chose science. I wanted to be an engineer, so I took maths. Janvi wanted to be a doctor, so she took biology. The pressure on us was the same. If anything, it was more on her.

The nagging about her studies never stopped. Keep reading. No spare time. No time to relax. If he caught her without a book in her hands, the lecture started all over again. And always, the same reminder. He needed a doctor in the family.

Some days, it was too much for her. When she could not hold it any longer, she cried.

Those were the years she needed her father most. Twelfth grade decides where a whole life goes. A father is supposed to be the shade a child can rest under in those years. Janvi needed shade. Our father gave her more sun.

> बड़ा हुआ तो क्या हुआ, जैसे पेड़ खजूर ।
> पंथी को छाया नहीं, फल लागे अति दूर ॥
> — *Sant Kabir, Sakhi*
>
> **Translation:** "So what if you have grown tall, like a date palm? It gives the traveller no shade, and its fruit hangs far out of reach."

Many children in India learn this doha in school. Kabir is talking about people who grow big and are no use to anyone. A date palm stands tall over everything around it. But a tired traveller on the road gets no shade from it. Its fruit hangs so high that nobody can reach it.

My father stood tall in our Samaj. People called on him. People looked up to him. Inside his own house, the one traveller who most needed his shade got none. And his approval hung far out of her reach.

I did not cry the way she did. That was not because the pressure on me was lighter. I had learned to swallow it. Janvi was gentler than me, and hers came out as tears. He never understood that difference between his two children. Maybe he never cared to.

I have come to believe something about my father. I think he enjoys pressing down on people who cannot push back. I think it gives him a kind of joy. Years ago, it was his own father. All through their marriage, it was his wife. Now it was his daughter. I don't think he ever saw it that way. He never realised that this time, the person under his weight was his own child.

> दुर्बलार्थं बलं सृष्टं धात्रा मान्धातरुच्यते ।
> अबलं तन्महद्भूतं यस्मिन्सर्वं प्रतिष्ठितम् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Chapter 92, Verse 11*
>
> **Translation:** "The Creator made strength for the sake of the weak, O Mandhata. So it is said. The weak are something great. Everything rests on them."

The sage Utathya says this to King Mandhata, in a long lesson on how a king should rule. In the middle of it, he makes a simple point about power. Strength does not exist for the strong. It exists to protect the weak. A few verses later, he gives the king a warning. The eye of the weak, he says, is as hard to bear as the eye of a sage or of a snake. It can burn a man and his whole family. "Never attack the weak," he tells him. Then he says it again.

Inside our house, my father was the king. I wrote in the last chapter that home was the one place where his word was final. By Utathya's rule, all of that strength was meant to protect the weaker people under his roof. His daughter most of all. He used it on her instead.

---

Even with all that weight on her, Janvi cleared her twelfth-grade board exams with good grades. Next came NEET.

NEET is the national entrance exam for medical colleges in India. It is one of the most competitive exams in the world. The year Janvi first sat it, more than thirteen lakh students appeared. The whole country had only about eighty-three thousand MBBS seats. That is roughly one seat for every sixteen students. Only about half of those seats were in government colleges, where the fees are far lower. The rest were in private colleges, where the fees run into lakhs every year.

Janvi was one of those thirteen lakh students.

It was 2020. COVID had shut the country down. The exam was pushed back twice, from May to July, and then to September. For Janvi, that meant months more of waiting. It also meant months more of him.

The closer the exam came, the harder he pressed. She was not allowed to go out. She was not allowed to relax. Her only job was to study. He called her many times a day from work to check on her. His nagging and her breakdowns came closer and closer together.

He kept reminding her how expensive medical college would be if her marks were not good enough. "Do you know what a private seat costs?" he would ask. "If you don't get a government seat, where will that money come from?"

Finally, the day came, and she sat the exam. I know she gave it everything she had.

---

It was not quite enough.

NEET is not a pass-or-fail exam. It is a merit list. Every student gets a rank, and seats go to the highest ranks first. Janvi's rank was good. It was just short. She missed the government seats for general-category students by a few marks.

She was heartbroken. Being a doctor was her dream, and now she might have to give it up. Her reason to be upset was real.

My father was disappointed too. He was furious with her. But his reason was different. He was thinking about his image. He could already hear what people would say. "Look at Shambu's daughter. She was so smart at school, and she couldn't even get into medical college."

Nobody had actually said it. No other student her age in our Samaj had even sat for NEET. It was too hard, so they never tried. Janvi tried. She came within a few marks of a government seat. For my father, that was not enough.

> पार्थ नैवेह नामुत्र विनाशस्तस्य विद्यते ।
> न हि कल्याणकृत्कश्चिद्दुर्गतिं तात गच्छति ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 6, Verse 40*
>
> **Translation:** "Partha, there is no ruin for him, not in this world and not in the next. No one who does good, my dear one, ever comes to a bad end."

Just before this verse, Arjuna asks Krishna a worried question. What happens to a person who tries with all his faith, but falls short of the goal? Is he lost, like a torn cloud that drifts apart in the sky? Krishna answers with comfort. Nothing is lost. No honest effort ends in ruin. And in the middle of his answer, Krishna calls Arjuna *tata*. It is the soft word an elder uses for a child. My dear one.

Janvi had tried with everything she had, and she had fallen short by a few marks. She needed one person in that house to say what Krishna said. Your effort is not wasted. You are not lost. My father prayed to Shiva every morning and every evening. He knew his scripture. He did not say one gentle word to her.

---

What he said instead was, "Take a drop."

Taking a drop means sitting out a year. The student stays home, studies for twelve more months, and sits the exam again. My father wanted Janvi to do exactly that, and try again for a government seat.

Janvi did not want to. She still wanted to be a doctor. But she was ready to change her path rather than spend another year at home, going through the same torture. There were other courses in the medical field. She could take one of those. She told him so, again and again.

"Papa, I'll take another course. Please. I can't do one more year like this."

"Another course? And what will I tell people? Everyone knows you are going to be a doctor."

He kept forcing her. He cursed at her. He thought about his image, and about the Samaj. He did not care what his daughter wanted. He did not see how much she cried. He did not care about her health, or her peace of mind. He cared about one thing only, and it was his name in the Samaj.

In the end, she was exhausted, and she gave in. She agreed to take a drop, the one thing she never wanted.

I know that feeling. Three years later, I would give in to him the same way, over a phone line from Halifax. I wrote it in the last chapter in two words. I lost. Janvi lost first.

---

The year that followed was the hardest of her life. I saw it happen. My father did not.

He went to work every day except Sunday. He was not home to see what I saw. I saw Janvi give that year everything she had. She read day and night, without a break. She cut off her friends. She had no social life. She gave her mind no rest at all. There was only one thing in her life, and it was study.

My father never watched any of it. He never noticed it. A father who saw that much effort might have tried to carry some of the weight. He did the opposite. He took his lectures to a new level. His calls to her each day doubled. The nagging grew. So did his expectations. So did his taunts.

He began to treat her like a burden. "If you fail again, then what?" he would say. "What will I tell people then?" There was no kind word. No "you can do this." Only pressure, from a man who was never home to see the work.

I have tried to imagine that year from inside her head. One of the hardest exams in the world. A quiet girl who had been afraid of her elders all her life. A voice on the phone all day, telling her she was not enough. I don't know if I would have made it through. I am not sure many people would.

More than once that year, Janvi said she couldn't take it anymore. More than once, she said she wanted to end her life.

Janvi wants this in the book. She wants it heard.

Our mother did not let it happen. She was with Janvi through all of it.

What my mother did for her that year, I cannot fully put into words. I can only write down what I saw. She sat up with Janvi when she studied late into the night. She brought tea and food to her desk, so Janvi would not skip a meal. When Janvi broke down, my mother held her until it passed. On the worst nights, she slept beside her. Again and again, she told her the same thing. "Whatever happens in this exam, you have me. Nothing in this world is worth more than you."

She did all of this while she was afraid of the same man herself.

> नास्ति मातृसमा छाया नास्ति मातृसमा गतिः ।
> नास्ति मातृसमं त्राणं नास्ति मातृसमा प्रपा ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Chapter 258, Verse 29*
>
> **Translation:** "There is no shade like a mother. There is no refuge like a mother. There is no protection like a mother. There is no water for the weary traveller like a mother."

This verse comes from the story of Chirakari. I quoted him once before, in the second chapter, on what a father is. I said then that his story did not end there. Here is the rest of it.

Chirakari's father, the sage Gautama, was angry with his wife. In that anger, he ordered his son to kill his own mother, and he went away. Chirakari was slow by nature. His name means "the one who is slow to act." So he sat and thought for a long time. First he weighed his father's word. Then he weighed his mother. This verse is part of that second weighing. Two verses earlier, he says something more. A mother protects her child whether that child is capable or not. While he was still thinking, his father came back in tears. His anger had cooled, and he was sorry for the order he had given.

Kabir's date palm gave the traveller no shade. Janvi found her shade anyway. It was our mother. On that long, hot road, she was the shade, the refuge, and the water. She asked for no marks in return. She would have protected her daughter whether she passed or failed.

In the third chapter, I quoted another verse from the same part of the Mahabharata. There is no refuge like a wife. This one says there is no refuge like a mother. The two verses are built the same way. In our house, they were about the same woman.

And the verse this book is named after does not begin with the father. Its first words are *Matru devo bhava*. Treat your mother as a god. The mother comes first. That year, I understood why.

Gautama came home crying, sorry for his anger. My father came home from work every evening that year. He never once came home like that.

---

Finally, that year ended. In September 2021, Janvi sat NEET for the second time. This time, she scored well. It was enough to get into medical college.

It was not enough for a seat in Surat. Her seat was at Zydus Medical College, in Dahod. The college runs in partnership with the Government of Gujarat, and Janvi had won a seat in the government quota. Dahod is on the eastern edge of Gujarat, near the border with Madhya Pradesh. It is a long way from Surat.

Janvi was happy. She had made it into medical college, even with all that pressure on her head.

Two people in our house were not happy. My mother was sad because her daughter would be living so far away. That was only natural. My father was unhappy for a different reason. He was about to lose his control over her.

"You are not going to study there," he told her. "Who will look after you? Away from me, with all that freedom, you will ruin your life."

By then, I was in Halifax. I had started my master's in January 2022. I could not be in the room. I fought for her the only way I could, over the phone. In the last chapter, I wrote that the cage had followed me to Canada, and that it rang. Now I used the same phone line to fight him for my sister.

Something had changed in Janvi too. For the first time in her life, she pushed back. She raised her voice. She would take this seat in Dahod, she told him, or she would not go to medical school at all.

He did not like that. He started pushing her to take a second drop, and to sit NEET for a third time.

This time, Janvi had had enough. She knew exactly how hellish the last year had been. She refused him, openly. Once again, she was ready to let go of her dream. She was not ready to give him another year of her life.

> सा होवाचाहं वै त्वा याज्ञवल्क्य
> यथा काश्यो वा वैदेहो वोग्रपुत्र उज्ज्यं धनुरधिज्यं कृत्वा
> द्वौ बाणवन्तौ सपत्नातिव्याधिनौ हस्ते कृत्वोपोत्तिष्ठेदेवमेवाहं त्वा
> द्वाभ्यां प्रश्नाभ्यामुपोदस्थाम् । तौ मे ब्रूहीति । पृच्छ गार्गीति ॥
> — *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, Chapter 3, Brahmana 8, Verse 2*
>
> **Translation:** "She said, 'Yajnavalkya, a warrior of Kashi or Videha strings his loosened bow, takes two sharp arrows in his hand, and rises to face his enemy. In the same way, I have risen to face you with two questions. Answer them.' He said, 'Ask, Gargi.'"

The Brihadaranyaka is one of the oldest Upanishads. In it, King Janaka of Videha holds a great debate. The wisest men of the land gather to question the sage Yajnavalkya. One of the questioners is a woman, Gargi Vachaknavi. Earlier in the debate, she presses him with question after question. Yajnavalkya stops her. "Gargi, do not ask too much," he says, "or your head will fall off." And Gargi goes quiet.

But she comes back. Later in the same debate, she tells the assembly she has two more questions for him. They let her ask. Then she turns to Yajnavalkya and says these words. She is a woman in a hall full of men, and she compares herself to a warrior stringing his bow. This time, Yajnavalkya does not silence her. He answers with two words. "Ask, Gargi."

Janvi had been silenced once. The year before, she had given in and taken the drop. Now she stood up a second time, with her bow strung. More than two thousand years ago, an Upanishad recorded a woman standing up to the strongest voice in the room. It recorded her with respect. It did not call her disrespectful.

My father did not say, "Ask, Janvi."

---

His answer was his hand.

When she kept refusing, my father raised his hand to hit her. He raised it at his *own* daughter. At the Lakshmi he showed off in the Samaj. At a child who could not take any more torture from her own father.

I was not there. I heard it afterwards, on the phone, from my mother and my sister.

My mother stopped him.

It matters who stopped him. In the third chapter, I wrote about the years he hit my mother in front of us. Janvi was small then. She would cry and beg him, "Papa, please. Don't hit Mummy." It never worked. Now his hand was raised at Janvi, and it was my mother who stopped it. The woman he had hit for years was the one who stopped his hand.

In that same chapter, I quoted Vidura. People take the one who forgives to be weak. My father had taken my mother for weak for more than twenty years. I wrote that he would find out one day how strong she was. This was the day.

Then he turned on both of them. He spoke ill of them. He cursed them, my mother and my sister together, with the same kind of words he had used on my mother for years.

> विद्याः समस्तास्तव देवि भेदाः स्त्रियः समस्ताः सकला जगत्सु ।
> त्वयैकया पूरितमम्बयैतत् का ते स्तुतिः स्तव्यपरा परोक्तिः ॥
> — *Devi Mahatmya (Durga Saptashati), Chapter 11, Verse 6*
>
> **Translation:** "All forms of knowledge are parts of you, O Devi. So are all women, everywhere in the world. You alone, the Mother, fill this whole world. What praise could ever be enough for you?"

The Devi Mahatmya tells how the goddess Durga destroyed the demons that even the gods could not defeat. After her final victory, the gods sing her this hymn. Every Navratri, all of Gujarat dances for the Mother for nine nights, and these verses are recited in her honour.

The gods say two things in this verse. All knowledge is a form of the Goddess. And all women are forms of the Goddess. Not some women. Not only the women of a good family. All of them.

Janvi was both halves of that verse. She was a girl, and she had given her whole young life to learning. By this verse, she was the Goddess twice over. Our house had welcomed her as Lakshmi. Nineteen years later, the man of that house raised his hand at her. And it was another woman, her mother, who stopped him.

This time, my mother and I stood with Janvi. My mother stood with her inside that house, and I stood with her from the other side of the world. We accepted the seat at Zydus.

---

Janvi did not wait. She was free of his tyranny at last. She packed her bags right away and left for Dahod. She did not want to stay in that house one day longer than she had to.

In India, a girl usually leaves her parents' home when she gets married. In many families, it is a day of tears. Janvi left hers at 19, for a medical college at the far end of Gujarat. And she was glad to go.

She was not happy because of the freedom. She had never asked for freedom. She was happy because, for the first time in years, there would be no pressure and no nagging from her own father. She could rest a little, before the hard years of medical school began.

The Lakshmi of our house had left it.

> तेष्वेवमादीनाचारानाचरत्सु विपर्यये ।
> नाहं देवेन्द्र वत्स्यामि दानवेष्विति मे मतिः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Chapter 221, Verse 79*
>
> **Translation:** "When they lived in these ways, against everything that is right, I made up my mind. 'O king of the gods, I will not live among the Danavas.'"

In the Shanti Parva, the goddess Lakshmi appears before Indra, the king of the gods. She tells him she has left the Danavas, the demons, and she explains why. She had lived with them for ages, as long as they held to truth and dharma. Then they changed. She lists what she saw. Among other things, desire and anger ruled them. They stopped looking after their children. Quarrels went on day and night, in house after house. So she made her decision, in this verse. She would not live there any longer.

At the start of this chapter, I quoted a hymn that asks Lakshmi never to leave. In the Mahabharata, Lakshmi herself explains when she does. She leaves a house where anger rules, where the children are not cared for, and where the fighting never stops. Ours left for the same reasons.

---

My father did not see it that way. He saw something to post on Facebook.

"My daughter cracked NEET." "My daughter got admission in medical college." The same Facebook where the Samaj had watched his passport fill with stamps now showed off his daughter.

Hypocrisy, again. This was the man who had told her she would ruin her life in Dahod. This was the man who had raised his hand at her when she chose it. Now he wanted the credit for her seat. He wrote it into his own column, like every other entry in that good name.

Other parents were paying crores to get their children into medical college. Private colleges. Management quotas. Colleges in other states. My sister won a government-quota seat with her own effort, for a fraction of that cost. It was a great achievement. It was hers. It was not my father's.

In his mind, of course, she got there because of him. Because of his pressure. Because of his calls. That is not what happened. She got there in spite of him.

For a little while, Janvi was happy. She was far away from him, and she could breathe.

It was not going to last. Medical school would be hard in its own way once her studies began. And further down the road, something even bigger was waiting for her.
