# Chapter Three: The Money He Wouldn't Take

My sister and I were not the only ones in that house who were afraid of him. I said as much near the end of what came before, and then left it alone. My mother was afraid of him too, in the particular way a person is afraid of someone she cannot leave and still has to cook dinner for. This chapter is hers.

Her story starts where everyone's in this family starts. In the same house.

After Sukhdev papa died, that house was still full. My parents. My sister and me. My uncle, Praful Patel, his wife, and their two children. My grandmother. One roof, one kitchen, the way it had been since before I was born. We had buried a grandfather and nothing else had moved. The joint family held. So did the man at the centre of it. He was exactly who he had always been.

---

The years right after were, by every measure the street would use, the best this family ever had.

Pal Gam stopped being a village while I was still a boy. The city reached it and kept going, and one day the maps had Surat where the fields used to be. When a village becomes a city, everything it sits on changes price. The land my family had always held around Pal, the same land Sukhdev papa had bent over his whole life for very little, was suddenly worth more sitting empty than it had ever been worth planted. Money came in that nobody had worked a harvest for. We went from lower middle class to something comfortably above it. Upper middle class. The kind of family that owns more than it uses.

My father had grown up counting every rupee twice before he let it go. Now he didn't have to. That is the part of this chapter I still turn over the most. He was handed room to breathe, and he did not spend a single inch of it on the people in his house.

---

While Sukhdev papa was still alive, my father had already begun sending his parents abroad.

It mattered to him that they fly. Neither of them had ever been on a plane. Most people their age on Moti Fali never would be. He wanted his mother and father, a housewife and a farmer who had raised him with almost nothing, to stand in an airport with tickets in their hands. I went with both my grandparents to the UAE. My father took his mother and father to Thailand. There were other trips after that, to other countries, more than I kept track of.

I have wondered since how much of it was for them and how much of it was for the street. A farmer's son from Pal Gam, flying his uneducated parents to Bangkok. In that community, in that decade, almost nobody had done it. It was the kind of thing the Samaj noticed and passed along. Shambu took his parents abroad. Whatever else it was, it was also that.

The trips I took changed me. I stood in places that ran on rules I had never been taught, and I came home holding a few of them. New ways to think about money, about women, about what a family owes the people inside it. You travel and you carry something back, or there was no point buying the ticket. My father travelled more than I did. More countries, more airports, more ways of living, seen with his own eyes. Did any of it change him? I have looked hard for what he brought back. I have never found it. His mind left Surat and returned exactly the size it had been.

---

Some things about him did not move no matter what the bank balance said.

He would not spend money. Not a rupee more than he had to, not on anything. There was one exception and it never varied. He paid our school fees without an argument, on time, in full, every year. His own education had cost him more than he ever explained to us, and he was not going to let his children start where he had started. On that, I have never had a bad word for him.

Everything else, we got somewhere else. Our clothes came from my mother's sister. She was kind, she had married into money, and she liked doing it, so year after year the shoes and the shirts and the small things a growing child needs came from her hands and not my father's. He never once seemed troubled by this. Why would he be. It was less for him to pay for. He had grown up going without, and going without had left him certain that need was simply normal, and someone else covering it was a small piece of luck.

My sister and I only named the pattern later, once we were old enough to see its shape. He paid the school fees, and then he stepped out of the frame. Everything after that, the actual raising of us, the daily cost of two children, the doctor and the clothes and the thousand small decisions, became our mother's department. He had done his part. He would have told you so himself.

There is a kind of father this country produces in large numbers. He pays for the education, takes the credit for it at every wedding, and hands the rest of the child to the mother. My father was that kind. He believed it was enough. He believed it was correct.

---

The money grew. Two things in him did not shrink to match it.

His anger was one. His closed fist was the other. The richer the family got, the clearer it became that neither had ever had anything to do with money in the first place.

> त्रिविधं नरकस्येदं द्वारं नाशनमात्मनः ।
> कामः क्रोधस्तथा लोभस्तस्मादेतत्त्रयं त्यजेत् ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 16, Verse 21*
>
> **Translation:** "Three are the gates of this hell, and each one destroys the self: desire, anger, and greed. A person should let go of all three."

Earlier in this book I set another verse from the same text beside my father, the one about anger clouding the mind until the reason is gone. This one names the company anger keeps. Anger and greed, listed side by side, two of the three doors Krishna says a man should turn and walk away from. My father walked toward both his whole life and called it discipline. He had only chosen forms of them the Samaj would never think to fault him for. His greed came out as thrift, and a poor man's son is praised for thrift. His anger, for years, came out on his own father's body. After that it had one place left to land.

It would be wrong to say the beating started when Sukhdev papa died. It only lost its old target. My father had been raising his hand to my mother across all the same years he was raising it to his father. Now she was the only person left in the house he was willing to hit. Any argument could arrive there. A late meal. A wrong word. A tone he did not like. The size of the disagreement never seemed to set the size of his answer. As far as I could ever tell, the logic underneath it was simple and fixed. He was right, always, about everything. A wife was staff. You do not lose an argument to the staff.

---

I am not going to lay out every incident. I could not if I wanted to. There were more of these than there ever were of the other kind, spread over more years, and I have lost most of the reasons he gave at the time. What stayed is the pattern. He slapped her. He hit her on the body. Some nights he used a belt. He said things about her parents and the home she came from that I will not put on the page, the same kind of words he had used on his own father, aimed now at the woman he had married. It happened in the bedroom. It happened in the main room. Once it happened in a hotel in Goa, on a family trip, in front of relatives, because the place was never the point. The anger was the point, and it did not care who was in the room.

> सम्राज्ञी श्वशुरे भव सम्राज्ञी श्वश्र्वां भव ।
> ननान्दरि सम्राज्ञी भव सम्राज्ञी अधि देवृषु ॥
> — *Rigveda, Mandala 10, Hymn 85, Verse 46 (Surya's Bridal Hymn)*
>
> **Translation:** "Be a queen over your husband's father, a queen over your husband's mother, a queen over his sister, a queen over his brothers."

That verse is not from a philosophy lecture. It is from the wedding itself. It is one of the oldest things the tradition says out loud, spoken over the bride while the fire is still lit, telling her that the house she is walking into is hers to rule, that she enters it as a sovereign and not as a servant. My father took pride in knowing his scripture. He had stood through that exact ceremony. Then he brought a wife home and spent thirty years making certain she understood she was the lowest-ranking person under his roof.

I want to be plain about what I think, because my father never was. Being the stronger person in the room does not make you the right one. Your wife being unable to hit you back is not a point in your favour. A disagreement between two adults does not have a winner who gets to use his hands. None of this is complicated. I understood it as a child. He was a grown man with a law degree and he never did.

---

One Sunday holds all of it together.

My mother was up first, the way she was every day. By the middle of the morning the house was swept, the washing was done, and lunch was cooked and covered, enough for everyone, his parents and his brother's family and mine. She had changed her clothes to go out. Her brother was coming to collect her.

Her father had sold a piece of land. He was an old man, and he wanted to put money into his daughters' hands himself, while he was alive to watch them take it, rather than leave it to be argued over later. He was glad to do it. There was nothing shameful in it. There is no rule, anywhere, in any book, that says a woman may not accept a gift from the house she was born in. The money was hers. Not the family's, not my father's. Hers.

My father would not allow it.

Not because we needed nothing. Because of what Moti Fali would say if word got out. "People will say I cannot provide for my own house. People will say I live off my wife's father. Only a man with nothing takes money from his in-laws." He said these things to her in the main room that morning, louder each time, while her cooked lunch went cold under its lids and her brother waited in the lane. Then he hit her. On a Sunday, in front of his children, over money that was never going to touch his hands.

> यत्र धर्मो ह्यधर्मेण सत्यं यत्रानृतेन च ।
> हन्यते प्रेक्षमाणानां हतास्तत्र सभासदः ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Chapter 93, Verse 48*
>
> **Translation:** "Where righteousness is struck down by wrongdoing, and truth by a lie, while the assembly sits and watches it happen, that assembly goes down with it."

The Mahabharata offers that line as a rule about any gathering of people, and every reader's mind goes to the same room: the hall where Draupadi was dragged and shamed while the elders, the teachers, and the whole court stayed in their seats and found reasons not to stand. The epic does not let those men off. It says the watchers fell along with the crime. There was a hotel room in Goa with relatives sitting in it. There was our main room on a Sunday with two children in the doorway. I was one of them. I knew, standing there, that what I was watching was wrong, in the complete and simple way a child knows things. I said nothing. There is one man in that epic hall who got to his feet and objected while everyone louder than him shouted him down. His name was Vikarna. I was not even Vikarna. I was a boy who watched, and stayed quiet, and carried it out of that room.

She left with her brother. She took the money. She was right to, and it is one of the few times in this story I get to say that plainly about anyone.

---

Here is how that money ended.

Years down the line, the same money my father would not be seen accepting became the money he lived on. Quietly, without ever announcing it, he stopped putting his salary into the household. The groceries came out of my mother's account. The vegetables, the milk, the gas cylinder, the medicines, the doctor, her own clothes, our school-day pocket money, the occasional meal bought from a restaurant. Her father's land, turned into cash, turned into the daily running of my father's house, a little more every month. He kept his own pay. A working man with a steady accounting job had handed the entire upkeep of his family to his wife's inheritance, and felt, as far as anyone could see, completely fine about it.

> कर्मेन्द्रियाणि संयम्य य आस्ते मनसा स्मरन् ।
> इन्द्रियार्थान्विमूढात्मा मिथ्याचारः स उच्यते ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 3, Verse 6*
>
> **Translation:** "The man who holds his hands still from action while his mind sits turning over the very things he claims to have given up is fooling himself. He is called a hypocrite."

Krishna is describing someone who makes a show of renouncing a thing and goes on wanting it in private. He is not talking about a household budget. The mechanism is the same one. My father had built a whole life out of things he was seen to refuse. Meat. Alcohol. Cigarettes. A single rupee of help from any person, because needing help was for the weak. In public the refusal was total. In private it was undone, on his terms, whenever it suited him, and the person it cost was always my mother. The Gita has one word for a man whose outside and inside do not match. I did not reach for it to wound him. It is simply the accurate one.

My mother never once put it to him as a debt. I do not think the thought ever formed. She believed she was taking care of her family, and taking care of her family was the entire content of her day, so the money left her hands without a second look. That is how she is built. She has spent her life certain that whatever she gives will not be counted against her later. She married the one kind of man who counts everything and returns nothing, and she never adjusted to him.

---

He never sent us out of the room. Not once. Whatever it did to a child to watch this, year after year, was not a factor he weighed. My sister, small, would cry and say it out loud. "Papa, please. Don't hit Mummy." It went nowhere. It was like speaking to a shut door.

So we stopped waiting for a different father to arrive. We had met the real one, and we arranged ourselves around him the way you arrange yourself around weather. We moved further back from him every year. By the time we were teenagers there was no warmth left in the bond to protect, only distance and a constant low reading of his mood. We did not love him. I want to be exact about that, because it is not a thing a son says lightly. We feared him, and the fear had crowded out everything a son is supposed to feel, and he had done that himself, over years, on purpose. He never tried to be loved by his children. He tried to be obeyed by them. How far he was prepared to take that, and what it looked like when it was pointed straight at my sister and me, is its own chapter further on.

Both of us still carry it. Years later, in our own homes, a raised voice at the wrong hour can put either of us right back in that main room. My sister says the same. That is the inheritance he actually left us. Not the land.

---

The calendars changed. Nothing else did.

I moved into higher secondary school, then college. He went on hitting my mother on the occasions he decided called for it, as though I were still too small to matter in the room. I was not. Somewhere in those years I began answering him. I raised my voice back. I told him, in words, that he was wrong, that this was not a thing a husband was allowed to do, that he had to stop. He was not used to being spoken to that way inside his own house, and it did not move him an inch.

One night I put myself between them and understood, clearly, that if he took one more step toward her I was going to put him on the floor. I meant it completely. He saw that I meant it. Even then, with his grown son squared up to him over it, nothing in his face said he had finally grasped the size of what he had spent twenty years doing. It genuinely had not reached him. Anger came first for him. Ahead of his wife, ahead of both his children, ahead of everything. It always had.

And the whole time, out on the street, he was still the educated one. The modern one. The steady, decent, self-made man the Samaj sent for when it needed someone reliable at the front of a hall. That tie only pulled tighter as the years went. The more of himself he spent at home on rage, the more he seemed to need the Samaj's good opinion to hold. The two things grew together.

---

My mother forgave him every time. She never stopped calling him her husband. She never once sat down and totalled what he owed her, in money or in years or in the plain safety a person is supposed to have inside her own bedroom. The account stayed open and one-sided for the whole of her married life, and she was at peace with that in a way I still am not.

The Samaj had a word for my father. Ideal. We had watched a different man, in rooms the Samaj was never allowed into.

The one bill he was ever handed in the open, a gift from his wife's family that might have embarrassed him in front of the street, he refused loudly enough for everyone to hear. Every hidden cost, the ones with no witnesses, he passed to her and let her carry. The money included. In the end he took all of it. He just made sure no one saw his hands.
