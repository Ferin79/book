# Chapter Four: The Son

I have written a lot about my father's hands. What they did to his father. What they did to my mother. How careful he was that nobody outside our house ever saw them.

Before I write what those hands did to me, I owe them one fair page. The same hands paid for almost everything I ever learned.

---

In the last chapter, I said there was one thing he paid for without an argument. Our education. That open hand reached further than school fees.

He sent my sister and me to an English medium school, when most children on Moti Fali went to Gujarati medium ones. After school, he paid for tuitions every afternoon. He never cut back on either one. Whatever else he refused us, he never refused us that.

He pushed me into karate, and he pushed me hard. If I am honest, that choice was partly mine. Maybe forty percent mine, and the rest his. I trained for years. I climbed four or five belts up from white. Because of him, I travelled to international tournaments in Sri Lanka and in Nepal. I could not tell you now exactly what I won there. I can tell you that I went, and that I went because of him.

He gave me a dream, too. Canada was his dream first. Long before it was mine, he sat the IELTS exam again and again, and he applied for permanent residency in Canada and in the United States. It never worked out for him. Somewhere in my childhood, his dream became mine as well. When the time came, he supported me all the way. He sent me to Canada for my master's degree. Today I live in Halifax as a permanent resident. I live in the country he could not reach.

He also gave me a clean life. I have never smoked a cigarette. I have never had a drink. I wrote about that rule in the second chapter, and I said then that I had never disagreed with him on it. I still don't. That lesson came from him.

Education. Karate. A dream, and the means to reach it. A life free of addiction. I will always be grateful for these. I mean that.

> माता शत्रुः पिता वैरी याभ्यां बाला न पाठिताः ।
> सभामध्ये न शोभन्ते हंसमध्ये बको यथा ॥
> — *Chanakya Niti, Chapter 2, Verse 11*
>
> **Translation:** "A mother is an enemy and a father is a foe when they do not educate their children. Such children do not shine in any gathering. They stand out like a crane among swans."

Chanakya is hard on parents who skip this duty. He does not call them careless. He calls them enemies. By that measure, my father was never my enemy. He made sure I would never stand in a room like a crane among swans. I want that on the record before I write anything else in this chapter. It is true, and this book only works if it tells the whole truth.

But there is another truth next to it. Sometimes I feel these were only a father's basic duties. He did them, with a little extra effort. Paying for a child's education is a duty. It is not the same as raising that child. And it is not the same as knowing him.

---

My father never knew me. There was never a real connection between us.

Ours was the usual relationship between an Indian father and his son. The son is afraid of the father. It does not matter what the situation is. The fear comes first, and everything else has to fit around it.

He supported my early education, but he was strict about the results. He always wanted the best grades possible. His thinking was simple. "I am sending you to an English medium school. I am paying for tuitions every afternoon. What reason do you have for bad grades now?" I think every father expected the same thing in those years. In that, he was not unusual.

What he never understood was balance. A child needs to study. A child also needs to play. To him, only the first one counted. If he came home from the office and found me watching TV, he did not like it. To him, a child in front of the TV was a child wasting his time.

> युक्ताहारविहारस्य युक्तचेष्टस्य कर्मसु ।
> युक्तस्वप्नावबोधस्य योगो भवति दुःखहा ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 6, Verse 17*
>
> **Translation:** "For the one who is balanced in eating and in recreation, balanced in his effort at work, and balanced in sleep and in waking, yoga becomes the destroyer of sorrow."

Krishna does not tell Arjuna to work without rest. He puts recreation in the same line as food, work, and sleep. The Sanskrit word is *vihara*. It means play, leisure, time spent for its own sake. Krishna treats it as part of a balanced life, not as a prize a child earns after the grades come in. And the god who spoke this verse had the most playful childhood in our whole tradition. Every Gujarati child grows up on stories of Krishna stealing butter and playing with the cowherd boys of Gokul. My father prayed every morning and every evening. He never seemed to notice that his own tradition lets children be children.

It was not only the TV. He did not like it if I got a haircut he had not approved. He did not like it when I chose something for myself. Thinking back, I cannot find one thing I chose that he liked. He never liked anything.

That was the real pattern. From childhood, I was never given an option for anything. I was supposed to do things his way, or the way he liked. My opinion did not matter. My choices did not matter. When I was small, the choices were small too. A haircut. A TV show. They hardly seemed worth fighting over. But it never changed. Not in my teenage years. Not when I was an adult. The choices only got bigger. His hold on them never loosened.

---

That hold was tightest on Sundays.

For most children, Sunday is the best day of the week. No school. No tuitions. A whole day that belongs to them. It was not like that for my sister and me. Sunday was my father's day off too. He was home all day. That meant we had to behave in front of him all day. We couldn't watch TV. We couldn't do anything silly, the way children do. Every hour had to pass his inspection.

He made the whole house tense. We felt it all day long. My sister and I hated him on Sundays, and we hated Sundays too. In the last chapter, I wrote about the Sunday he beat my mother over her father's money. That was the worst Sunday. It was not the only hard one. Every Sunday was hard.

I don't think he knew how to be a father in the first place. Not a good father. Not even an ordinary one. My sister and I had watched what he did to his own father. We had watched what he did to our mother. So we learned to fear him very early, and that fear has never left us. We always believed he might lash out at any time, for any reason he decided was wrong. We could never tell what that reason would be. So we never relaxed. Not once.

> यस्मान्नोद्विजते लोको लोकान्नोद्विजते च यः ।
> हर्षामर्षभयोद्वेगैर्मुक्तो यः स च मे प्रियः ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 12, Verse 15*
>
> **Translation:** "The one from whom the world does not shrink back, and who does not shrink back from the world, who is free from elation, resentment, fear, and agitation, that one is dear to Me."

Krishna is describing the kind of person he loves. The first thing he names is not prayer. It is not fasting, or knowledge of scripture. It is a person that other people do not shrink from. My father knew his scripture. He prayed to Shiva twice a day, every day. And inside his own house, two children shrank from him every Sunday of their childhood.

That fear was not imagined. He hit me too. Many times, at every age, right up until I finished twelfth grade. I cannot give a list of reasons. Most of the time there was no reason worth giving. It was the same anger my mother knew, and my grandfather too. And through all those years, he kept hitting my mother as well.

He never once thought about what this does to a child. He never asked what a child carries out of a house like that. I don't think the idea ever entered his head. He did not know how to be a father. He only knew how to be obeyed.

No special bond ever grew between us. My sister and I still call him Papa. We call him that because we have to. We don't have an option there either.

---

And Papa had one lecture he never got tired of giving.

I used to call them his monthly sessions. That is an exaggeration. They came far more often than once a month. The subject never changed. Don't smoke. Don't drink. They are harmful. They destroy a person's life. Focus only on your studies. And always, at the end, the same example. "Look at your grandfather. Look how he went because of it."

I did not need that example explained to me. I was there the night Sukhdev papa died. I was the one he woke when his chest went wrong. I was the one who ran through the house to wake everyone else. I also knew what my father had done to that same man's body, for years, over the drinking. Now my father was using his death as a warning for me. In all those sessions, he never once mentioned the rod.

The lesson itself was right. I said so at the start of this chapter. I understood it the first time he said it. Honestly, I understood it before he ever said it. But how many more times did I need to hear it? Come on. After a while, it stopped feeling like advice. It stopped feeling like guidance. It felt like torture.

Underneath it, I could see what it was really about. He did not trust his own son. He cared more about his image than about trusting me. He had a name to protect in the Samaj. He wanted the Samaj to see how ideal a son he had raised. Many boys my age in our Samaj were not doing well in school. His son had to be the example. A son who smoked, or drank, or failed his exams would have been a crack in the image. So he kept repeating the lecture, as if repeating it could keep the image safe.

---

The older I got, the harder he guarded that image. My teenage years were the hardest years with him.

I was growing up. I was starting to think for myself, and I had my own opinions on things. He was still living in the 1800s. He was narrow-minded. Any opinion of mine that did not match his was a mistake he had to correct.

> लालयेत्पञ्चवर्षाणि दशवर्षाणि ताडयेत् ।
> प्राप्ते तु षोडशे वर्षे पुत्रे मित्रवदाचरेत् ॥
> — *Chanakya Niti, Chapter 3, Verse 18*
>
> **Translation:** "Pamper a son for five years. Discipline him for the next ten. Once he reaches sixteen, treat him as a friend."

Chanakya is not a soft teacher. The word he uses for those middle ten years is *tadayet*. It means to strike. He allowed a father the rod, and I am not going to pretend he didn't. But even Chanakya, a teacher famous for being hard, put an end date on it. At sixteen, a son becomes a friend. My father's hand did not stop at sixteen. It stopped after twelfth grade. And the friendship never came at all. Not at sixteen. Not at twenty-two. Not now.

Instead, there were new sessions. No girlfriends. No *lafda*. In Gujarati slang, a lafda is an affair, the kind of trouble people whisper about. He said plainly why it mattered. A lafda would be too bad for his image in the Samaj. That was the main reason. He said so himself.

Not that I wanted a girlfriend. That was never the point. The point is what it shows about him. This rule came from a man who had travelled to ten different countries. A man who had once planned to settle abroad himself. He had seen with his own eyes how people live in other places. He came home still living in the 1800s, and he held his son back there with him.

> पोथी पढ़ि पढ़ि जग मुआ, पंडित भया न कोय ।
> ढाई आखर प्रेम का, पढ़ै सो पंडित होय ॥
> — *Sant Kabir, Sakhi*
>
> **Translation:** "The whole world died reading book after book, and not one person became wise. Whoever reads the two and a half letters of love becomes wise."

Kabir was a weaver, not a scholar. He spent his life telling scholars that their books had not made them wise. The word for love, *prem*, is written with just two and a half letters. Kabir says that one short word teaches more than a whole library. My father had read a great deal. He had a law degree. He had taught himself English. He had ten countries behind him. He read all of it, and he never learned those two and a half letters. If he had, he would have trusted his son.

The rules kept coming. I did not have my own phone until I finished twelfth grade. Even after that, if he saw me on my phone, the nagging started. No games on the computer. No games on the phone. No talking to friends for long. No nights out. Not even a night away to study with friends. Why? Because he could not trust his own son. It was that simple, and that sad.

I could only buy the clothes he approved of. I could only wear what he liked, and he decided what suited each event. If my beard or my hair grew even a little, he nagged me to trim it. If I went out in the evening and was not home by ten, the calls started. Ten calls. And anger, every single time.

No freedom. No choice. No life. Just do what he says, and he will be happy. He never asked for my opinion on anything. To be honest, I was living in hell.

I used to look at the fathers of my friends, boys my own age. Those fathers were completely different from mine. I felt that difference badly, many times. In my father's mind, a child outside the house had to be up to something bad. There was no other explanation. It was a stressful way to live, and it went on for years.

My sister and I grew up. He did not. He never changed his behaviour. He still got angry at everyone, including us. My sister's rules were even stricter than mine. Her story belongs to the next chapter, and I will give it all of that chapter.

By the end of school, that house felt like a cage. I wanted out of it.

---

Finally the time came, and I flew out of the cage.

In January 2022, I started my master's in computer science in Halifax, Canada. I had come to study, and to build the life I had dreamed about for years. I was 22. I thought all the misery was over. I thought I was finally free.

I was wrong.

The lectures followed me across the ocean. I was 22 years old, studying for a master's degree on the other side of the world, and I was still getting the talk about smoking and drinking. Really. At 22.

He still wanted to run my life from Surat. His morning was my night, and the calls came anyway. "Why are you awake so late?" "What were you doing?" "Why didn't you pick up my call?"

I had flown halfway around the world. The cage had simply come with me. Now it rang.

---

Then the calls turned to the biggest decision of my life.

Marriage is the biggest dream of almost every Indian parent. Here is the paradox. It is supposed to be the child's own life, and nobody cares what the child wants. My father was only waiting for my master's degree to finish. It finished in June 2023. Soon after, five biodatas came to me. Five girls. I was to choose one of them.

No one asked what I preferred. No one asked whether I even wanted to get married now. It was simply the right age in our caste. In his mind, it was my time, and he could not wait even a few years. It was not a suggestion. It was force.

> इति ते ज्ञानमाख्यातं गुह्याद्गुह्यतरं मया ।
> विमृश्यैतदशेषेण यथेच्छसि तथा कुरु ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 18, Verse 63*
>
> **Translation:** "I have now explained to you this knowledge, more secret than any secret. Reflect on it fully, and then do as you wish."

This line comes near the very end of the Gita. Krishna has taught Arjuna for almost the whole book. He is God, speaking to a man on a battlefield, about the biggest decision of that man's life. And when he is done, he does not order Arjuna to obey. He tells him to think it through, and then to do as he wishes. God gave Arjuna a choice. My father prayed every morning and every evening. He would not give his own son a choice about his own wife.

The calls came every day after that. Every call was about the girls he had chosen for me. I was not ready, so I pushed back. He did not like that at all.

His calls had a pattern. He always started politely.

"Ferin, this is the right age for marriage. Everyone your age is already engaged. In our caste, this is the right time. Look at me. I got married at twenty-two. This is right for you too."

I would answer him with reasons. "Papa, this is not your time. You got married at twenty-two in a completely different decade. Please come out of it. Change your thinking. Stop caring about the Samaj. I don't care what the Samaj or the caste thinks about me."

When he felt he was losing, the politeness ended. He started scolding me. "Don't teach me. I'm your father. You will get married where I say, and when I say."

Every time, I was left with no choice.

> युक्तियुक्तमुपादेयं वचनं बालकादपि ।
> अन्यत्तृणमिव त्याज्यमप्युक्तं पद्मजन्मना ॥
> योऽस्मत्तातस्य कूपोऽयमिति कौपं पिबत्यपः ।
> त्यक्त्वा गाङ्गं पुरस्थं तं को नाशास्त्यतिरागिणम् ॥
> — *Yoga Vasishtha, Book 2, Chapter 18, Verses 3–4*
>
> **Translation:** "Words that stand on reason should be accepted, even from a child. Anything else should be thrown away like straw, even if Brahma himself said it. A man has the Ganga flowing right in front of him, and still he drinks from a well, because 'this is my father's well.' Who would not scold a man so attached?"

The Yoga Vasishtha is the teaching the sage Vasishtha gave to Rama when Rama was still a young man. A guru, speaking to a prince. And the guru says reason should win. It should win even when it comes from a child, and even against the word of a god. My father's last argument was never a reason. It was a rank. "I'm your father." That was all he had left. And "Look at me, I got married at twenty-two" was his father's well. The Ganga was right in front of him. A new decade. A new world. Ten countries, seen with his own eyes. He chose the well anyway, and he wanted me to drink from it too.

The scolding got worse. He used foul language on his own son. He forced my mother to convince me. He used foul words on her too. And when words did not work, he beat her, to make her convince me.

My mother was sick of him by then. All she was doing was thinking about what was good for her son. She was not even really taking my side. She was taking the side of my future. Her mind was far more modern than his. It was far more modern than the Samaj's, too. And she was the one who got beaten for it.

> संरोहति शरैर्विद्धं वनं परशुना हतम् ।
> वाचा दुरुक्तं बीभत्सं न संरोहति वाक्क्षतम् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Vidura Niti), Chapter 34, Verse 75*
>
> **Translation:** "A wound from arrows heals. A forest cut down by the axe grows back. But a wound made by cruel, ugly words never heals."

Vidura said this to King Dhritarashtra, on a night the king could not sleep for worry about the war his own family was heading into. Vidura's point is simple. A body heals. Words do not. I have written about my father's words before. The words he used on Sukhdev papa. The words he used about my mother's family. Now they came down the phone line, at me and at my mother, day after day. A beating heals. Vidura says words don't. I believe him. I can still hear those calls.

In the end, I gave up. I could not keep fighting that war over the phone every day. I had a job to do for the rest of the day. On the other side of the world, my mother was being beaten by her husband because of me, and no one was there to help her. So I stopped fighting.

I lost.

---

Once I stopped fighting, he was free to choose. Here is how he chose.

He had one filter. Only one. "She should be from a rich house." That was it.

It did not matter if a girl from a less wealthy family was better in every other way. To him, the richer her family was, the better our status in the Samaj would be. He could already hear what people would say. "Look where Rakesh got his son engaged. Into such a well-known family."

In our Samaj, a well-known family means a wealthy family. A family is known by the money it has, not by the values it holds. A richer family is automatically a well-known family. It automatically has better status, and it automatically counts as an excellent household. Only money matters. That is a disease of our whole Samaj, not only of my father. It deserves a book of its own. I cannot fight every battle in one chapter, so let me stay with him.

My father was educated. He had travelled the world. He had built himself up from nothing. If anyone in our family could have broken this chain, it was him. He was not bound by the Samaj's rules. Nobody could have forced him to follow them. He followed them anyway. Then he forced me to follow them too, because he believed his way was the right way.

> यद्यदाचरति श्रेष्ठस्तत्तदेवेतरो जनः ।
> स यत्प्रमाणं कुरुते लोकस्तदनुवर्तते ॥
> — *Shreemad Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 3, Verse 21*
>
> **Translation:** "Whatever a leading man does, other people do the same. Whatever standard he sets, the world follows."

Krishna says this to explain why a respected man has to act with care. People are watching him. Whatever he does becomes the rule for everyone else. In our Samaj, my father was that man. I wrote about it in the first chapter. He was the one they called on. He was the one they called ideal. If he had chosen a girl for her character, people would have noticed. Some of them might have followed. He set a different standard instead. He chose by money, in front of everyone, and he called it the right thing to do.

A girl from a rich family is not automatically a good girl. She is not automatically the right girl for me, either. None of that mattered to him. "A really good girl from a lower family is fine," he would argue. "But she is not suitable for our family." That is how backward his thinking was, even in 2023.

---

I saw how deep it went in one story. It started with a phone call.

I was in Canada, busy with my software job. It was a normal morning. My phone rang, and it was him. "There is a girl for you. Her name is Vrunda."

That was all he told me about her. Nothing else. Then came a ten-minute speech about her family. How good they were. How well known they were in the Samaj. Her father had a big business exporting mangoes. Her elder sister was married into a big family. On and on it went.

When he finally stopped, I asked him one question. "Am I marrying her family, or the girl?"

That question said everything. For him, the family's status was the only thing that mattered. In ten minutes, he had not told me one thing about the person I was supposed to marry.

> स होवाच पितरं तत कस्मै मां दास्यसीति ।
> द्वितीयं तृतीयं तं होवाच मृत्यवे त्वा ददामीति ॥
> — *Katha Upanishad, Chapter 1, Valli 1, Verse 4*
>
> **Translation:** "He said to his father, 'Father, to whom will you give me?' He asked a second time, and a third. His father said to him, 'To Death I give you.'"

The Katha Upanishad opens on a father putting on a show. Vajashravasa is performing a great sacrifice. He is giving away everything he owns, for the reward it will earn him. But the cows he gives away are old and dry. They have drunk their last water and given their last milk. His young son, Nachiketa, watches the show and sees straight through it. He asks the only honest question left. "Father, to whom will you give me?" He asks three times. His father, angry now, gives his answer. "To Death."

My question on that call was the same question. Who are you giving me to? A girl, or a family's name? Vajashravasa gave away worn-out cows to look generous. My father was ready to give away his son to look important. Right before that verse, the Upanishad says where a man who gives such gifts ends up. In joyless worlds.

---

I talked with Vrunda for a couple of weeks. Then we paused our conversation until later that year, when I was going to India for a month. In between, I was made to talk online with many other girls. All of them were from even richer families.

I hated the idea of that trip. I knew I would come back engaged to one of these girls. I just didn't know which one.

When I landed, he had already planned five girl viewings. Vrunda was one of them. I met all five girls. None of them interested me much, except Vrunda.

But a couple of the other families were better known than Vrunda's. So now he pushed me to choose one of those girls instead of her, because their families were richer and had better status.

This was pure madness. When I was in Halifax, he had sung songs about Vrunda's family. Now that I had chosen her, he wanted me to choose someone else, because the other families were even better.

Hypocrite.

That was the death of choice. I lost all faith in him. I realised then that I was suffering, in my own mind, from the Samaj's disease. My father carried it, and it was no longer only harming him. By now it was harming everyone around him. I am ashamed to call him my father. I saw his true face that week, and I cannot unsee it.

> दीर्घायुरथ वाल्पायुः सगुणो निर्गुणोऽपि वा ।
> सकृद्वृतो मया भर्ता न द्वितीयं वृणोम्यहम् ॥
> — *Mahabharata, Aranyaka Parva, Chapter 278, Verse 26*
>
> **Translation:** "Whether he lives long or dies young, whether he has virtues or none, I have chosen my husband once. I will not choose a second."

Savitri says this to her own father, King Ashvapati. Her story starts with him. When no suitor came for her, he did not choose for her. He told her to go out and find a husband herself, one equal to her in qualities. Qualities, not wealth. She chose Satyavan. His father was a king who had gone blind and lost his kingdom. The family lived in a forest hermitage. They had nothing left. Then the sage Narada brought terrible news. Satyavan would die within a year. Even her father faltered. "Go," he told her, "choose another." She answered him with this verse. And her father accepted her choice.

Married women across Gujarat still keep a fast in Savitri's name every year. Her story is not obscure. The difference between her father and mine is plain. Ashvapati had a real reason to doubt. His daughter's husband was going to die. My father's reason was that another family had more money. Ashvapati still let his child choose.

My father did not.

---

But this time, he was not the only voice in our house.

I was lucky. My family stood behind me. My mother stood with me. My Kaki, my uncle's wife, stood with me. My sister stood with me.

I think about that often. In the last chapter, I wrote about the two women who ran our house as its unpaid servants. My mother and my Kaki. In that house, neither of them was ever supposed to have a say in anything. Now those two women, and my sister, stood up to him for me.

They argued with reasons. Valid, logical reasons. He could not keep up with them. For once in that house, reason won.

In December 2023, I got engaged to Vrunda. She is going to be my wife soon.

I am lucky to have her. She is a good person, with a good character. But I know how lucky I was. Not everyone is.

---

There are many fathers in India with the same mindset as mine. They force their sons and daughters to marry into the families they like. Many of those marriages fail. Many end badly, for one person or for both.

I was lucky in the end, but the decision was still never really mine. The biggest decision of my whole life was made by my father. I was never given a chance to explore. I had about a week to choose the girl I will spend my whole life with.

This is what I want this chapter to show. How deep the disease runs in our Samaj, and how deep it runs in my father.

In our Samaj, a son's marriage is not about the son. It is about what people will say. A girl is measured by her father's business. A family is measured by its money. A father's pride is measured by how rich a family he can marry his son into. Nobody stops to ask whether the two people will be happy together. The father learned it from the Samaj. The Samaj learned it from the generation before. Each father hands it down to his son as if it were dharma.

And the men who could change it choose not to. Educated men. Travelled men. Men the Samaj looks up to. My father was one of those men. He had everything he needed to break the chain. He chose to tighten it, and he tightened it around his own son.

> मातृदेवो भव । पितृदेवो भव । आचार्यदेवो भव । अतिथिदेवो भव ।
> यान्यनवद्यानि कर्माणि तानि सेवितव्यानि । नो इतराणि ।
> यान्यस्माकं सुचरितानि तानि त्वयोपास्यानि । नो इतराणि ॥
> — *Taittiriya Upanishad, Shiksha Valli, Anuvaka 11, Verse 2*
>
> **Translation:** "Treat your mother as a god. Treat your father as a god. Treat your teacher as a god. Treat your guest as a god. Whatever deeds are blameless, those you should follow. Not the others. Whatever good conduct you see in us, that you should honour. Not the rest."

These are the words a teacher spoke to his student on the day the student finished his studies and went out into the world. Almost every Indian child has heard the first lines. Treat your father as a god. My father would have liked me to stop reading there. But the teacher keeps going, in the same breath. Follow only the blameless deeds. Honour only our good conduct. Not the rest. More than two thousand years ago, the Upanishad already knew that fathers and teachers have faults. It does not ask the son to copy them. It asks him to choose.

So I am choosing.

I keep what was good. The education he paid for. The clean life he taught me. The dream of Canada that was his before it was mine. I carry those, and I am grateful for them.

The rest stays with him. The fear. The control. The lectures that were really about his image. The Samaj's disease, and the idea that a person is worth the size of her father's business. I will not pass any of it on. The chain he chose not to break ends with me.

He spent my whole life choosing for me. This part, I choose.
