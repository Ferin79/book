# Writing Rules for This Book — Narrative Non-Fiction / Memoir

This document defines the guidelines for writing and reviewing chapters of this book: a true story about the author's father and family. Read this file whenever drafting or reviewing any chapter — it is the core rule set for how real events get written as narrative.

## 1. Voice & Tone
- **Warm yet Honest**: Write with the curiosity and storytelling energy of a skilled biographer/memoirist — someone who clearly loves the subject but doesn't flatter him. Avoid the dry, distancing tone of a report, obituary, or encyclopedia entry.
- **Human-Centric Narrative**: A life doesn't unfold in a vacuum. Focus on the people, their real struggles, real relationships, real disputes, and the atmospheric context of the places they lived and worked.
- **Earned Emotion**: Build the reader's feeling through specific, concrete detail rather than telling them how to feel. Trust small real details — a habit, an object, a phrase someone always used — to carry weight.

## 2. Narrative vs. Reflective Tense

Use two registers to guide the reader between storytelling and reflection:
- **Past Tense (Narrative/Biographical)**: Use for all events, scenes, and biographical description.
  - *Example*: "In the summer of 1994, he took the overnight train alone for the first time."
- **Present Tense (Reflective/Explanatory)**: Use when the author steps back to explain a lasting pattern, a general truth about the family, or a piece of context the reader needs.
  - *Example*: "That habit — checking the lock twice before bed — never left him."

## 3. Concrete Detail Over Abstraction

- **The Visual Grounding Rule**: Never explain an emotion or a life circumstance in the abstract when a concrete image can show it instead. Translate abstract feelings (grief, pride, worry, hope) into specific, physical, sensory moments.
  - *Example (Worry)*: Instead of "She worried about money constantly," show her recount the same rupee notes twice before putting them away.
  - *Example (Pride)*: Instead of "He was proud of his son," show him keep a single newspaper clipping folded in his wallet for years.
- Avoid generic emotional language ("it was a difficult time," "he felt overwhelmed") when a specific real detail is available or can reasonably be asked for.

## 4. Biographical Weaving (Anti-Info Dump)

- Do not dump pages of static biography before getting to what happened. Weave background details into the timeline of events as they become relevant.
- Introduce someone's history, quirks, or circumstances at the moment they influence what's happening in the scene, not all at once in an introduction paragraph.
- Make the setting a character: describe the ambient sound of a specific house, the smell of a specific kitchen, or the particular quiet of a specific commute to ground the story in physical reality.

## 5. Avoid Retroactive Anachronism

- **The Timeline Rule**: Never describe an earlier period using knowledge, technology, terminology, or attitudes that only existed later. Don't attribute foresight, vocabulary, or context that the people in the scene couldn't have had at that point in time.
  - *Example*: If a scene is set in the 1980s, don't reference a mobile phone, an email, or a piece of modern slang.
  - *Example*: If someone hadn't yet received a promotion, title, or role at the point in the story, don't refer to them by that later title (see `references/author_rules.md` §6 for the full title-timeline rule).
- This applies to attitudes and context too — don't project the author's present-day understanding of an issue onto how a person actually thought or spoke about it at the time, unless the narrator is explicitly stepping outside the scene to offer that reflection.

## 6. Quoting Real Sources

- **Markdown Integration**: When quoting a letter, document, diary entry, or interview, format the source title in *italics* and weave direct quotes in as blockquotes (`>`) to mark them clearly as primary material distinct from narrated prose.
- **Narrative Framing**: Introduce quotes by conveying the tone in which they were written or said — formal, weary, joyful, guarded — so the reader understands the register before reading the words.
- **No Fabricated Quotes**: Never invent a quotation and present it as something a real person actually said or wrote. Reconstructed dialogue (see `references/author_rules.md` §0) is written as narrated dialogue in the prose, not formatted as a sourced quotation.

## 7. Quality & Fact-Proximity

- **Flagging Gaps**: If a specific biographical detail, date, or event is uncertain, disputed among family accounts, or simply unknown, do not invent it. Use the `[NEED RESEARCH]` flag in the chapter draft and record the query in `Research/research_tracker.md`.
- **Humility Over Drama**: Avoid calling a life event "destined" or a person's traits "larger than life" for effect. Let the small, specific, sometimes contradictory truth of a real person carry the narrative — that's what makes memoir land harder than invented drama.
- **No Hagiography, No Character Assassination**: Show real strengths and real flaws as they actually were. A memoir that only praises or only criticizes reads as untrustworthy; the honest, complicated truth is the more powerful story.
