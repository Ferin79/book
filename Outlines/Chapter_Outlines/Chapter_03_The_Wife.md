# Chapter 3: The Wife

*(Memoir outline — adapted from the fiction template; sections that assume an invented plot are omitted. Every beat maps to something real, per the author's account in `plan-3.md` and the author's direct answers during the 2026-09-28 revision.)*

## Chapter Summary

Opens Part III — The Husband. Picks up the thread Chapter 2 left dangling (Heena Patel also lived inside the fear in that house) and makes this chapter hers. Since the 2026-09-28 revision, the chapter reaches her life straight away instead of four sections in.

After Sukhdevbhai Patel's death and cremation, the joint family stays whole: nine people, one kitchen. Two women run that kitchen and the whole house: Heena Patel and Praful Patel's wife ("Kaki"). Nandanben Patel, Praful Patel, and Rakesh Patel all treat them as the house's unpaid servants. Rakesh goes further. He believes women are made for work and are under him, and he shows neither woman respect or care. Outside the house he is the opposite with women: polite, soft-spoken, helpful, quick to praise their cooking. The narrator reads it as kindness spent where the Samaj can see it.

Around those two women the family grows rich (Pal Gam absorbed into Surat, land values up, upper middle class), yet Rakesh spends none of it inside his own house. His spending goes where the Samaj can see it: flying his parents abroad. Travel changes the narrator, not his father. Two traits stay fixed whatever the bank balance. The first is his miserliness: school fees are the only willing expense, and Heena's sister ("Masi") covers the children's other needs. The second is his anger, which after Sukhdevbhai Patel's death has only his wife left as a target.

The chapter gives a representative account of the domestic violence, including a family trip to Goa in front of relatives, with the children almost always in the room. It centres on one Sunday: Heena, dressed to go with her brother to receive money from her father's land sale, is beaten purely over what the Samaj would say. Then comes the hypocrisy reveal. Years later Rakesh runs the household on that same inheritance and keeps his own salary. She had worked without a maid's pay; now she pays the bills too. The chapter closes on the cost to the children, the narrator's young-adult intervention, the public image tightening, and Heena's forgiveness. Rakesh "quietly left her to carry every cost that no one could see. Her work. Her money. Her safety."

## Key Beats (real, in order — reordered in the 2026-09-28 revision)

1. **Bridge from Chapter 2**: names Heena Patel as the other person in that house who feared him. Hers was a different kind of fear: she could not leave him, and she still had to cook his dinner every night (plants the kitchen). "This chapter is hers."
2. **The house**: after Sukhdevbhai Patel's death and cremation, the joint family is still whole: parents + Ferin + Janvi; Praful Patel + wife + two children; Nandanben Patel. Nine people, one roof, one kitchen. The man at its centre is unchanged.
3. **The two women who ran it (new, 2026-09-28)**: Heena Patel and Praful's wife, "my Kaki." Every detail below was confirmed by the author: up early before anyone else; swept, did the washing, cooked every meal for nine, served everyone first, ate last, washed up. No day off. The work was expected even when sick. The men never helped and never said thank you. Both women needed permission to visit their own parents or to spend money. To Nandanben, Praful, and Rakesh they were the servants of the house, not supposed to expect anything back. The maid comparison: a maid is paid monthly and gets a day off; they got neither, for years. Rakesh did more than expect it: in his mind women were made for work and made to be under him. He gave neither woman respect or care. To him they were part of the house, "like the stove or the water pot. Useful. Always there. Not something a man thanks."
4. **Outside the house (new, 2026-09-28)**: with women outside the family he was the opposite. On Moti Fali he greeted women warmly, spoke softly, and never raised his voice. He went out of his way to help with errands, favours, and problems, and praised women's cooking and work at Samaj functions and on visits. At home, two women cooked every meal he ate, for years, and the narrator cannot remember him thanking either once. Narrator's reading: "His kindness went where the Samaj could see it. Nobody from the Samaj was standing in our kitchen." [Scripture: Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva 46.5 — women must always be honoured; where they are not, every sacred rite is fruitless; the family whose women grieve is already lost. Analysis: Bhishma to Yudhishthira from the bed of arrows; verse 3 names the husband's brothers among those who owe women honour, so Rakesh stands in that relation to Kaki and Praful to Heena. His twice-daily prayer "bore no fruit." "From the street, ours looked whole."]
5. **Rising fortunes**: handoff "Around those two women, the family was getting rich." Pal Gam is absorbed into Surat city limits and the family's long-held land around Pal jumps in value, lifting the household from lower-middle to upper-middle class. "Inside his own house, he did not spend a single inch of it."
6. **International travel**: handoff "There was one kind of spending he never minded. The kind the Samaj could see." The trips began while Sukhdevbhai Patel was alive: Ferin to the UAE with both grandparents, Rakesh to Thailand with his parents, other trips after. The narrator reads them as partly for his parents and partly a Samaj performance ("Shambu took his parents abroad"). Travel changed the narrator (money, women, family); his father's "mind left Surat and came back exactly the size it had been. He flew home to the same kitchen, and the same two women working in it."
7. **Trait that didn't change (1): miserliness**. Handoff "Money was the clearest proof of that." At home he spends nothing beyond the minimum. The one exception is school fees, paid in full and on time (a genuine credit, tied to Ch 1's self-financed education). Clothes and small needs came for years from Heena's sister, "my Masi" (well-off, "a big heart to match," glad to help; the earlier "married into money" was cut at the author's request). He paid the fees and stepped out of the frame; the rest became the mother's department. The narrator names the type of Indian father who calls that "his part."
8. **Trait that didn't change (2): anger, now aimed at the wife**. Hinge: "His hand stayed closed, and his temper never cooled." [Scripture: Bhagavad Gita 16.21 — the three gates of hell: desire, anger, greed; explicit callback to Ch 2's Gita 2.62–63.] The beating predates Sukhdevbhai Patel's death and only lost its old target. Any argument could end with his hands: "A different opinion. A choice she made without asking him first. A tone he did not like." ("A late meal" was removed at the author's request.) "Every person has a mind of their own. He never allowed my mother hers" (from `plan-3.md`). "A wife was staff" now echoes beat 3.
9. **The catalogue (representative, not exhaustive)**: slaps, blows to the body, sometimes a belt, and words about her parents and natal home (not transcribed). It happened in the bedroom, in the main room, and on a family trip to Goa in front of relatives; the anger "did not care who was in the room." **Moved here in the 2026-09-28 revision**: the children were almost always in that room, he never sent them out, and Janvi's plea ("Papa, please. Don't hit Mummy."). [Scripture: Rigveda 10.85.46 — the bride as queen over her in-laws. Analysis adds "In our house, it ran the other way. The two brides who came into it were made to serve." "He had stood at that fire himself" replaces "that exact ceremony" (we cannot confirm this verse was recited at his own wedding), and "nearly thirty years" replaces "thirty years" (married December 1998).] The narrator's convictions follow, stated plainly.
10. **The Sunday (centrepiece scene, fully rendered)**: "My mother was up first, the way she was every day" calls back to beat 3. Chores done, lunch for the whole house cooked and covered ("his parents" removed, since it is unknown whether Sukhdevbhai Patel was alive by then). She is dressed to go; her brother is coming to collect her. Her father sold land and wants to give his daughters their share himself, gladly. "Like so much else, it needed my father's permission. He would not give it" calls back to beat 3. His reason is Samaj optics ("people will say I live off my wife's father"). He argues her down in the main room while her lunch goes cold and her brother waits, then hits her in front of the children. [Scripture: Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva 93.48 — the assembly that watches dharma struck down is destroyed with it. Tied to the Draupadi sabha, the relatives watching in Goa, the two children watching that Sunday, and Vikarna ("I was not even Vikarna").] She leaves with her brother and takes the money; she was right to.
11. **The hypocrisy reveal**: years later Rakesh stops putting his salary into the household and runs it on that same inheritance (groceries, vegetables, milk, gas, medicines, doctor, her clothes, the children's pocket money, the occasional restaurant meal), keeping his own pay. New line: "For years my mother had done a maid's work in that house without a maid's pay. Now she was paying the house's bills as well" (callback to beat 3). [Scripture: Bhagavad Gita 3.6 — मिथ्याचारः / "hypocrite." The analysis now makes clear that only his refusal of help was dropped in private. The old wording could be read as saying he secretly ate meat, drank, or smoked, which the book does not claim.] Heena gives without counting; she married a man who counts everything and gives nothing back, and she "never adjusted to him."
12. **The cost to the children** (the rest of the old "children" block, bridged from beat 11): "My sister and I had no choice but to." They stopped waiting for a different father and moved further back every year. They feared rather than loved him, and the narrator is exact that Rakesh sought obedience, not love. **Forward thread**: how far the control went, pointed at Ferin and Janvi directly, is named as its own later chapter (Part IV). Flashbacks persist "on our bad nights" (the author's own phrase from `plan-3.md`, replacing an unconfirmed "in our own homes"). "My sister says the same."
13. **Growing up / intervention**: higher secondary, then college. The narrator begins answering back. "Once" (not "one night") he puts himself between them, ready to put his father on the floor. Nothing in Rakesh's face shows he understands. Anger comes first. Publicly he is still the educated, modern, self-made man the Samaj relies on. New callback to beat 4: "so polite to the women on Moti Fali, and so quick to praise their cooking at a function." The tie tightens as private conduct worsens.
14. **Close**: "Through all of it, my mother forgave him. Every time." She never added up what he owed her: the money, the years, her safety. The Samaj's word for him was "Ideal"; the family watched a different man. Final lines: the one time his wife's family offered help that might embarrass him in front of the street, he refused it loudly. "He quietly left her to carry every cost that no one could see. Her work. Her money. Her safety. In the end he took all of it. He just made sure no one saw his hands."

## Thematic Elements

**Core theme(s)**: Money and exposure to the wider world changed nothing in him; the anger and the greed were never about scarcity. **Inside vs. outside**: courtesy and generosity spent where the Samaj could see them (women on the street, flights abroad), and none inside the house. The wife the tradition installs as sovereign of the household was made its unpaid servant, then its treasury as well. A rule framed as principle (refusing his wife's family's money) was really about reputation, and was abandoned the moment it could be abandoned in private. Fear as a deliberately maintained instrument of control, now shown in the marriage. The public "ideal man" image tightening in exact proportion to the worsening private conduct.

**Direct payoff / continuation**: Completes the "who pays the price" line running from Chapter 1 (planted) through Chapter 2 (Sukhdevbhai Patel's body). Here it is Heena Patel's work, inheritance, and body. Opens Part III.

**Internal callbacks (added in the 2026-09-28 revision to carry the flow)**: the kitchen (opening → beat 3 → travel → Sunday morning); permission (beat 3 → the Sunday); the maid's pay (beat 3 → the money reveal → "Her work" in the closing line); courtesy to women outside (beat 4 → the public-image paragraph).

**Callbacks to earlier chapters**: Chapter 1 — self-financed education / school-fees credit; twice-daily prayer; the Samaj "ideal man" (no meat/alcohol/tobacco, self-made, trustworthy); the closing "someone pays for it" figure. Chapter 2 — his verbal abuse of Sukhdevbhai Patel; "he never once sent us out"; the street's words vs. the children's words; "My sister says the same"; the earlier anger verse (Gita 2.62–63) now paired with Gita 16.21.

**Forward threads (do not resolve here)**: Rakesh's control of Ferin and Janvi directly (Part IV — flagged in prose as its own chapter). Heena Patel's fuller interior account of the marriage (rest of Part III).
