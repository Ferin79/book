### Social & Cultural Context

**Family Structure**: Rakesh Patel's household of origin — father Sukhdevbhai Patel (farmer), mother Nandanben Patel (housewife); lower middle class to poor. His own adult household — wife Heena Patel, son Ferin Patel, daughter Janvi Patel. **The family lived as a joint family under one roof, one kitchen, from before Ferin Patel's birth.** After Sukhdevbhai Patel's death (Ch 2) the joint household remained intact (Ch 3): Rakesh Patel + Heena Patel + Ferin + Janvi; Rakesh's brother Praful Patel + Praful's wife + their two children; and Nandanben Patel. Nine people, one kitchen. **(Ch 3 revision, 2026-09-28):** the two daughters-in-law, Heena Patel and Praful's wife (called "Kaki" by Ferin), ran the whole house as its unpaid servants. Nandanben Patel, Praful Patel, and Rakesh Patel all treated them that way. See their profiles. Heena Patel's natal family (her father, a sister, at least one brother) live elsewhere and appear in Ch 3 — see their profiles below.

**Cultural Elements**: Gujarati Hindu family; Gujarati calendar used for reckoning auspicious dates (Rakesh Patel's birth is dated in the family's memory both by Gregorian date and by its position in the Gujarati calendar — first Monday of Shravan month). Strong household observance of daily prayer (mornings and evenings) and devotion to Lord Shiva. The family belongs to the **Kadva Patel Samaj** — a community organization/social network for the Kadva Patidar (Kadva Patel) sub-caste originating from Gujarat, uniting members through cultural events, caste-specific matchmaking, and charitable work (including schools and hospitals). Confirmed by the author (2026-08-17): the entire village of Pal Gam, including the family's street Moti Fali, is Kadva Patel Samaj — every house and family on it belongs to this Samaj. The Samaj itself extends well beyond the village, across Patel families in Gujarat generally. Rakesh Patel is/was an active, well-regarded member. **(Street name corrected 2026-08-27 — see Notes at the bottom of this file. The street is Moti Fali; "Moti Fadiyu" was a mistaken correction made and then reversed. Always use "Moti Fali.")** **Funeral custom (author correction, 2026-09-28):** the family is Hindu. The dead are cremated, never buried. Never use burial language ("buried," "grave") for anyone in this family.

**Economic/Social Circumstances**: Rakesh Patel's childhood/teenage years were lived in poverty to lower-middle-class conditions; his parents were uneducated. He financed his own college education (law degree) against that backdrop and separately learned English, notable achievements for his background and era. As an adult he held a stable Accounting job in Surat and was known in the community for honesty and hard work. **(Ch 3):** in the years after Sukhdevbhai Patel's death the family's fortunes rose sharply — Pal Gam was absorbed into Surat's city limits, and the farmland the family had long held around Pal jumped in value, moving the household to upper-middle class. The narrator's framing: the extra money changed nothing in how Rakesh Patel treated his household. He remained extremely tight with money — the one expense he covered without argument was his children's school fees (paid in full, on time, every year); the children's clothing and most day-to-day needs were covered for years by Heena Patel's sister. Separately (Ch 3), Rakesh Patel later stopped contributing his salary to household expenses altogether and ran the entire household on Heena Patel's inheritance while keeping his own pay — see both profiles below.

#### Rakesh Patel
- **Relationship to Author**: Father; central figure of the book
- **Life Dates**: Born 2 August 1976 (Monday; first Monday of Shravan month, Gujarati calendar), Pal Gam, Surat
- **Occupation**: Accounting (stable job, Surat); trained as a lawyer (completed a law degree despite his family's poverty and his parents being uneducated)
- **Nickname**: "Shambu" — given by everyone in his *fadiyu*/*fali* (lane/street) in Pal Gam; not a family nickname but a neighborhood one. Note the resonance: Shambhu is a name of Lord Shiva, and Rakesh Patel is a committed Shiva devotee (prays mornings and evenings) — whether the name was chosen with that in mind or is a coincidence of the Shravan-Monday birth is [TBD, worth asking the author].
- **Public Persona**: Presents as modern, devoutly Hindu, and caring toward everyone in his social/community circle; known throughout his community for honesty and hard work
- **Private Reality (per author and sister's account)**: Prioritized personal pride and social standing over his duties as a husband and father; specific incidents to be documented as they're gathered
- **Background**: Born and raised in Pal Gam, Surat. Childhood and teenage years spent in poverty to lower-middle-class conditions. Son of Sukhdevbhai Patel (farmer) and Nandanben Patel (housewife), both uneducated. Struggled to finance his own college education because of this; completed a law degree and separately learned English, both notable achievements for his background and era (per the author's account of his father telling this story).
- **Marriage & Children**: Married Heena Patel on 10 December 1998. Son Ferin Patel born 9 December 1999; daughter Janvi Patel born 11 November 2002. By the time his family was "complete" (two children, one wife), he also held a stable Accounting job in Surat.
- **Ambition — North America**: Held a longstanding interest in foreign countries and settling in North America. A work trip to Hong Kong, China gave him direct exposure to life abroad and accelerated this ambition. At around age 30 he attempted the IELTS exam several times and applied through permanent-residency programs in both the USA and Canada. He did not get the opportunity he was hoping for. He remained content with what he had built in Surat regardless.
- **Children's Education**: Ensured Ferin and Janvi were admitted into English medium school rather than the Gujarati medium school most families on the street used — a deliberate, unexplained decision, framed by the author as a direct throughline from his own self-taught English/law degree.
- **Standing in the Samaj**: His own street (Moti Fali) and the whole village of Pal Gam are entirely Kadva Patel Samaj, but his standing extended well beyond that — across the wider **Kadva Patel Samaj**, the community organization/social network for the Kadva Patidar (Kadva Patel) sub-caste across Gujarat, which unites members through cultural events, caste-specific matchmaking, and charitable work including schools and hospitals. Regularly called on to help organize or attend community weddings, funerals, and festivals. Publicly perceived as an ideal man: does not drink alcohol, does not smoke, is fully vegetarian, and is considered trustworthy — this reputation is described by the author as something that took a lifetime to build and was maintained at an (as yet unspecified) ongoing cost.
- **Relationship to the Samaj was mutual, not just imposed**: Per the author's direction (2026-08-17), the chapter also shows this wasn't only the Samaj calling on him — he actively sought out involvement (the next function, the next committee, the next festival), and did so uncritically: he did not appear to weigh whether a given Samaj custom or expectation was actually good for his family, trusting instead that if everyone else treated something as simply how things were done, it was right for his own house too. The author was explicit that this should read as a hint about him, **not** as criticism of the Samaj as an institution — keep it framed that way in any future chapter that revisits this.
- **[NARRATIVE THREAD — RESOLVED in Chapter 2]**: Chapter 1 ended by stating that maintaining his status/pride/importance in the Samaj required some ongoing, unnamed sacrifice. Chapter 2 ("The Father," Part II) resolves this: the cost was never his own to pay. It was extracted from his father, Sukhdevbhai Patel, whose drinking (unlike his own past smoking) Rakesh treated as an unacceptable threat to the family's reputation and marriageability within the Samaj — see Sukhdevbhai Patel's profile below for the incident record.
- **Violence against his own father (documented in Chapter 2)**: Circa the 2010s, rather than pursuing any available treatment for Sukhdevbhai Patel's alcoholism (de-addiction centers, medical care, community/religious counsel — all real options at the time), Rakesh Patel, together with his brother Praful Patel, responded with sustained physical violence against their father. Per the author's account (`prompt-2.md`): more than thirty separate incidents over several years, including tying Sukhdevbhai Patel to the bed when passed out, beating the soles of his feet with a rod, slapping/beating him elsewhere on his body, throwing cold water on him in winter to force him conscious, and burning his feet with hot utensils. Verbal abuse (severe curse words) accompanied the physical violence. The violence escalated over time rather than easing, and Rakesh showed no visible guilt or acknowledgment afterward. Occurred openly at home, witnessed directly by his children (Ferin and Janvi), not hidden from them.
- **Aftermath**: Sukhdevbhai Patel died of a heart attack in the middle of the night (exact date unknown — `[NEED RESEARCH]`); Ferin Patel was with him when it started and woke the household. Both Ferin and Janvi grew afraid of their father from this period on; the author states Rakesh knew this and used the fear to control his children and, in her own way, Heena Patel too (her specific experience is reserved for a later chapter, per the author's own framing). The author states that if asked to describe their father, he and Janvi would say "fearful, angry" — directly contradicting the Samaj's view of him (honest, hardworking, devoted, trustworthy) established in Chapter 1.
- **Wealth without change (documented in Chapter 3, "The Wife")**: after the family rose to upper-middle class on rising land values, Rakesh Patel's conduct toward his household did not change. Two fixed traits, per the narrator, independent of money: (1) **miserliness** — he would not spend beyond the minimum; the sole willing exception was his children's school fees (a genuine credit, tied to his own self-financed education); the children's clothing and most daily needs were covered for years by Heena Patel's sister, which Rakesh accepted without discomfort because it reduced his own spending; and (2) **anger**, which after Sukhdevbhai Patel's death had Heena Patel as its only remaining target. The narrator also names a parenting pattern: Rakesh "paid the school fees and stepped out of the frame," leaving the rest of raising the children to Heena Patel, and considered that his complete duty done.
- **International travel as arranged by Rakesh Patel (Ch 3)**: he made a point of flying his uneducated parents abroad (Thailand with both of them; Ferin Patel to the UAE with both grandparents; further trips). The narrator reads this as partly genuine feeling for his parents and partly a performance for the Samaj ("Shambu took his parents abroad"). The narrator's pointed reflection: his own trips changed how he thought (about money, women, family obligation); his father travelled more and returned unchanged.
- **Domestic violence against Heena Patel (documented in Chapter 3)**: sustained over many years — the narrator states there were more incidents against Heena Patel than the 30+ against Sukhdevbhai Patel, and that the wife-abuse both predated and outlasted the father-abuse. Methods (representative/composite, per the author, not a claim of total recall): slapping, blows to the body, a belt on some nights, and verbal abuse aimed at her parents and natal home (same class of words used on Sukhdevbhai Patel, not transcribed). Occurred in the bedroom, the main room, and once on a family trip to Goa, in front of relatives (the exact setting within Goa is not established; the 2026-09-28 revision removed an unconfirmed "hotel"). Always in front of the children — he never sent them out. Janvi Patel, as a child, would cry and beg him to stop ("Papa, please. Don't hit Mummy."), to no effect.
- **The Sunday incident (Ch 3, centrepiece)**: Heena Patel had done every chore and cooked the whole joint family's lunch, and was dressed to leave with her brother to receive money from her father (who had sold land and wanted to give his daughters their share in his lifetime — nothing shameful, no rule against it, the money hers alone). Rakesh Patel forbade it purely over Samaj optics ("people will say I live off my wife's father," "only a man with nothing takes money from his in-laws"), argued her down, then beat her in front of the children. She took the money anyway; the narrator states plainly she was right to.
- **The household-finance inversion / hypocrisy (Ch 3)**: years later Rakesh Patel quietly stopped putting his salary toward the household and ran the entire house on that same inheritance (groceries, vegetables, milk, gas, medicines, doctor, Heena's clothes, the children's pocket money, occasional meals out), while keeping his own pay. The narrator names him a "hypocrite" here — earned by this specific contradiction (public refusal of the money, private dependence on it), mirrored by Bhagavad Gita 3.6's मिथ्याचारः — used once, precisely, not as a general epithet.
- **Public image tightening as private conduct worsens (Ch 3)**: throughout, Rakesh Patel remained to the street/Samaj the educated, modern, decent, self-made, reliable man (callback to Ch 1's close); the narrator's observation is that his need for the Samaj's good opinion grew in proportion to how much of himself he was spending at home on rage.
- **The women of the house vs. women outside it (Ch 3, added in the 2026-09-28 revision, per the author's direct answers)**: at home he treated both daughters-in-law, his wife Heena Patel and his brother's wife ("Kaki"), as objects. In his mind women were made for work and were "under him." He showed neither woman respect or care, never helped with housework, and never thanked either of them; the narrator cannot remember him thanking them once, though they cooked every meal he ate at home. Outside the house he was the opposite with women. He was polite and respectful (greeted them warmly, spoke softly, never raised his voice) and helpful (went out of his way with errands, favours, and their problems). He also praised other women's cooking and work at Samaj functions and on visits. The narrator's reading, framed as his own interpretation: "His kindness went where the Samaj could see it."
